Executive Summary

1793-2025 From Universal to Rare to Resurgent

In January 2026, every 18-year-old male in Germany will receive a questionnaire. It will ask about their physical health, mental fitness, educational background, and willingness to serve in the armed forces. Women can volunteer to fill it out. By mid-2027, medical examinations will follow.

This is not conscription—yet. But it is infrastructure.

Germany joins a global pattern: After decades of treating military service as a rarity, nations worldwide are rebuilding the apparatus of mass mobilization. Not necessarily to draft millions tomorrow, but to have the data, systems, and legal frameworks ready if they need to.

Key Finding: Between 1990-2013, 24 countries suspended conscription. Since 2014 (Russia's Crimea annexation), at least 8 European nations have reintroduced, expanded, or begun planning mandatory service systems. This is the decisive end of the "peace dividend."

But the new conscription differs fundamentally from its historical precedent:

  • Historical: Mass levy → barracks → fixed service term → discharge
  • Modern: Registration → data collection → algorithmic assessment → modulated "readiness"

This document tracks the global state of conscription across 60+ countries, examines five case studies in depth, situates the trend within 230+ years of history, and analyzes it through the lens of control societies—where power operates not through enclosure (barracks) but through continuous data modulation.

Core Argument: Modern conscription serves dual purposes: overt (emergency mobilization capacity) and covert (justification for comprehensive biometric and behavioral data collection on entire youth cohorts). In an era where warfare increasingly involves drones, cyber operations, and privatized military contractors, the "draft" becomes less about bodies and more about data infrastructure.

This is not your grandfather's draft. It's an algorithmic mobilization system designed for rapid activation in crises while maintaining peacetime volunteerism.

Historical Context

From Ubiquitous to Rare: A 230-Year Arc

To understand why the current resurgence matters, we must grasp how historically abnormal the 1990-2014 period was. For most of modern history, military service was not a question—it was a given.

1793 — The Birth of Mass Mobilization

The French Revolution's Levée en Masse decree marked a fundamental transformation: defense shifted from the monarch's mercenaries to the citizenry's universal responsibility. All able-bodied, unmarried men aged 18-25 were requisitioned for immediate military service. The French army swelled to an estimated 800,000 active troops by September 1794—unprecedented in European warfare.

Significance: Established the principle that national defense is a civic duty, not a professional specialty. This model would dominate for the next two centuries.

1800s-1914 — Global Spread

Throughout the 19th century, conscription became standard across Europe and beyond:

  • Prussia: Universal male service (1814) created the model for German military power
  • Japan: Meiji Restoration (1868-1912) mandated service for modernization
  • Ottoman Empire: Introduced conscription for territorial defense
  • South America: Post-independence nations institutionalized service for border security

By 1900, military service for young men was nearly universal in industrializing nations.

1914-1945 — Total War Peak

World Wars I and II represented the apex of mass conscription:

  • WWI: ~70 million troops mobilized globally, majority conscripts
  • WWII: ~100+ million troops mobilized, with unprecedented industrial-scale organization
  • US Civil War (1863): First major American draft, controversial but foundational
  • Vietnam Era: US draft ended 1973 amid massive resistance, marking beginning of AVF shift

Significance: Conscription enabled "total war"—entire societies organized for sustained conflict.

1947-1991 — Cold War Ubiquity

The Cold War sustained conscription across both blocs:

  • NATO: Most members maintained universal or selective service
  • Warsaw Pact: All members required multi-year terms
  • Non-Aligned: Nations like India, Israel maintained large conscript forces

Conscription was considered necessary for credible conventional deterrence against peer threats.

1990-2013 — The Great Suspension

The collapse of the Soviet Union triggered the most dramatic peacetime demilitarization in modern history:

  • 24 countries suspended conscription between 1990-2013
  • European armed forces declined 16% average personnel (2010-2020)
  • Germany ended mandatory service in 2011, cutting forces by 25%
  • Rationale: No peer threat, expeditionary focus, cost savings

The Peace Dividend Logic: Professional, smaller, specialized forces were deemed more cost-effective for international crisis management than mass conscript armies for territorial defense.

2014-Present — Resurgence

Russia's annexation of Crimea shattered the post-Cold War consensus:

  • Lithuania reintroduced lottery conscription (2015)
  • Sweden reinstated selective service (2017)
  • Latvia expanded reserve systems (2019)
  • Croatia reintroduced 2-month basic training (2025)
  • Denmark extended conscription to women (2025)
  • Germany announced questionnaire system (2025 for 2026 implementation)

The New Logic: Peer threats have returned. Conscription provides depth that professional forces lack, while the infrastructure serves broader surveillance and control functions.

🇩🇪 Germany: The Questionnaire Infrastructure

Architecture of Data Collection
Hybrid System | Infrastructure-First Approach

Implementation Timeline:

  • January 2026: Questionnaire distribution to all males born 2008
  • Mid-2027: Medical examinations ("Musterung") begin

Theoretical Framework: Control Societies

Foucault's Biopower & Deleuze's Control Societies

The shift in conscription models reflects a broader transformation in how power operates in modern societies:

Disciplinary Society (1793-1990)

  • Enclosure: Barracks, training camps, fixed service terms
  • Discipline: Standardized training, hierarchical observation, normalization
  • Molding: Creating soldiers through repetitive practice and spatial organization
  • Example: 2-year mandatory service in fixed military units

Control Society (1990-Present)

  • Modulation: Continuous assessment, variable service requirements
  • Data: Biometric profiles, psychological assessments, skills inventories
  • Flexibility: Reserve systems, part-time service, selective activation
  • Example: German questionnaire system with algorithmic assignment

Key Insight: Modern conscription isn't primarily about gathering bodies—it's about gathering data. The infrastructure serves dual purposes: military readiness justification for comprehensive youth surveillance.

Agamben's State of Exception & Bare Life

Giorgio Agamben's concept of the "state of exception" helps explain how conscription systems operate in legal gray zones:

State of Exception

  • Suspension of Norms: Emergency powers that override normal legal protections
  • Preventive Measures: Infrastructure built during peacetime for wartime activation
  • Legal Ambiguity: Registration systems that exist in permanent "readiness" state

Bare Life (Homo Sacer)

  • Reduction to Biological Existence: Conscripts as bodies to be deployed
  • Datafication: Personal information becomes military property
  • Algorithmic Management: Human lives processed through assessment systems

Application: Modern conscription systems create a permanent state of exception where citizens' data can be appropriated for state purposes, reducing individuals to data points in military planning algorithms.
