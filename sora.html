From Telegraph to AI: 150 Years of Digital Colonialism

How Infrastructure Shapes Who Gets to Know

Essential Summary

The Pattern: For 150 years, communication technologies have been rolled out first to wealthy Western nations, then slowly to the Global South—creating compound disadvantages where each delay makes the next worse.

The Timeline: Telegraph cables (1850s) → Submarine internet cables (1990s) → Smartphones (2000s) → 4G networks (2010s) → AI tools like Sora (2024) — each arrived 5-15 years later in Africa, Asia, Latin America.

Why It Matters: Early users don't just get access first—they set the standards. Language defaults, cultural norms, what counts as "appropriate content," how algorithms learn—all shaped by whoever arrives first. By the time Global South users get access, they're adapting to systems designed for someone else's reality.

The AI Problem: Generative AI (ChatGPT, Sora, DALL-E) is trained mostly on English/European content. When OpenAI rolls out Sora to the US/UK first, early feedback makes it better at Western cinematography, Western faces, Western stories. When it finally reaches Kenya or Brazil, it's already optimized for elsewhere—and that gap becomes permanent in the training data.

The Compounding Effect: This isn't just unfair—it's cascading. Poor internet → late smartphone adoption → less video creation → underrepresented in AI training → worse AI tools → harder to compete globally → economic disadvantage → can't afford next-gen tech → cycle repeats.

1850s–1914 Telegraph networks built along colonial trade routes—London to Bombay, Cape Town, Sydney. Infrastructure designed for empire.
1990s–2000s Internet cables follow same routes. Africa gets <1% of global bandwidth despite 18% of population. Intra-African traffic routed through Europe.
2007–2015 Smartphones arrive. 4G reaches 95% of North America by 2015, but only 30% of sub-Saharan Africa by 2020. By the time access arrives, app ecosystems already mature.
2022–2024 ChatGPT, DALL-E, Sora roll out to US/Europe first. Models trained on 54% English content, 33% European languages, <0.5% African languages. Default behaviors set before global access.
2025+ AI becomes infrastructure for education, work, creativity. Early-access regions control standards. Global South adapts or is left behind.

1. Historical Infrastructure as Inherited Constraint

1.1 The Telegraph and Colonial Information Architecture (1850–1914)

The global telegraph network was explicitly designed to serve colonial administration. The Eastern Telegraph Company and similar entities laid submarine cables connecting London to Bombay, Cape Town, and Sydney—routes determined by imperial governance needs, not equitable information distribution.

This infrastructure established three lasting patterns:

  • Hub-periphery topology: Metropolitan centers became obligate nodes for intra-colonial communication, preventing direct South-South connectivity
  • Linguistic standardization: Codebooks, protocols, and training were standardized in European languages and administrative practices
  • Economic gatekeeping: Usage costs and access points were controlled by colonial entities, excluding indigenous populations

Key Insight: Independence movements inherited this infrastructure as given—technical, financial, and expertise barriers made wholesale replacement infeasible. Post-colonial states continued operating within communication topologies designed for their own subjugation.

1.2 Submarine Cable Networks and the Geography of the Internet (1980s–2000s)

The physical architecture of the internet recapitulates telegraph-era inequities with remarkable fidelity:

  • Cable routing asymmetries: As of 2024, Africa—home to 18% of global population—accounts for less than 1% of global bandwidth capacity
  • Latency penalties: Geographic distance from major internet exchange points imposes structural latency (200–400ms Cape Town–New York vs. 20–50ms London–New York) and bandwidth costs 5–10× higher
  • Path dependency: Upgrading requires capital investment feasible primarily for Northern-headquartered firms (Google, Meta, Microsoft), which retain proprietary control over undersea cable infrastructure

Intra-African internet traffic is routinely routed through European or North American exchange points due to lack of direct interconnection—a digital echo of colonial-era trade patterns.

1.3 Mobile Telephony and the "Leapfrog" Myth (2000s–2010s)

Mobile technology was heralded as enabling the Global South to "leapfrog" fixed-line telephony. Empirical outcomes reveal a more complex picture:

  • Temporal lag: Smartphones and 4G/LTE networks arrived 5–8 years later in sub-Saharan Africa than in OECD markets. As of 2023, 4G coverage reaches only 65% of Africa's population vs. 95%+ in North America/Europe
  • Device affordability barriers: Entry-level smartphones cost 50–150% of monthly income for median African users vs. 5–15% in high-income countries
  • Data cost stratification: Per-GB mobile data costs average $3–7 in sub-Saharan Africa vs. $0.50–2 in North America

Epistemic Consequence: By the time high-speed mobile internet became accessible in the Global South, content platforms (YouTube, Wikipedia, social media) had already crystallized around Northern user behaviors, languages, and cultural norms—effectively foreclosing participation in their formative design.

2. AI Rollout Strategies as Epistemic Enclosure

2.1 The Sora Paradigm: Staged Deployment as Structural Exclusion

OpenAI's Sora video generation model followed a release pattern now standard across frontier AI systems:

  1. Phase 1 (Feb–Aug 2024): Closed testing with U.S.-based filmmakers and institutions
  2. Phase 2 (Aug–Dec 2024): Invite-only access in U.S., Canada, UK, Western Europe
  3. Phase 3 (2025+): Gradual global expansion contingent on "regulatory compliance" and "content moderation infrastructure"

This sequencing is rationalized through three corporate logics, each obscuring deeper structural dynamics:

The "Regulatory Safety" Justification

Corporate claim: Early rollout in jurisdictions with "clear legal frameworks" (EU GDPR, U.S. DMCA) minimizes liability risk.

Concealed dynamic: "Clear" is a proxy for familiar to corporate legal teams—many Global South nations have robust IP and data protection laws (Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019, Brazil's LGPD), but lack of in-house expertise makes compliance costlier for corporations. Exclusion is driven by corporate convenience, not legal necessity.

The "Data Quality" Justification

Corporate claim: Deploying in "high-connectivity markets" yields better usage data for model improvement.

Concealed dynamic: This is tautological—models trained predominantly on Northern data perform better for Northern users, justifying further Northern-focused training. The feedback loop entrenches initial biases: Global South languages and cultural contexts are structurally under-represented in training corpora, leading to poor model performance, which is then used to rationalize continued exclusion from beta programs that could correct these deficiencies.

The "Incremental Risk Management" Justification

Corporate claim: Phased rollout allows iterative refinement before "more complex deployment environments."

Concealed dynamic: Complexity is framed as technical (connectivity, device heterogeneity) when it is fundamentally cultural and political—diverse linguistic norms, non-Western copyright regimes, postcolonial suspicion of Northern tech platforms. By deferring engagement with this complexity, corporations avoid the costly work of genuine co-design and instead export Northern defaults as universal standards.

2.2 The Training Data Colonization Problem

Generative AI models like Sora, GPT-4, and DALL-E are trained on datasets that:

  • Overrepresent Northern content: CommonCrawl (a major web scrape used in training) indexes predominantly English-language sites (54%) and European-language sites (33%), with African languages comprising <0.5%
  • Embed Northern visual culture: Sora's video generation is optimized for Hollywood cinematography, Western urban/suburban landscapes, and Northern body types—African, Asian, Latin American visual vernaculars are statistical outliers
  • Reproduce historical exclusions: Training data scraped from the internet inherits all prior layers of digital exclusion—sparse African web presence, low smartphone video creation, bandwidth constraints limiting video uploads

Epistemic Feedback Loop: AI trained on Northern data serves Northern users best → Northern users generate more AI content → next-generation models become more Northern-biased. Global South populations are locked into perpetual epistemic latency—always responding to norms set elsewhere, never shaping foundational paradigms.

3. Cascading Marginalization: How Sequential Exclusions Compound

The key insight is that each infrastructural layer does not merely replicate prior inequities—it amplifies them.

3.1 Temporal Capture Effects

Early adopters of any platform disproportionately shape its:

  • Default settings: Language options, content moderation rules
  • Recommendation algorithms: Trained on early user behavior
  • Cultural norms: What constitutes "appropriate" content, humor, reference points

By the time late-access populations join, these defaults are naturalized—perceived as technical necessities rather than contingent design choices reflecting specific user populations' preferences.

3.2 Compounding Infrastructure Deficits

Each new technology layer assumes prior infrastructure:

  • AI video generation assumes: high-bandwidth connectivity (for uploads/downloads), powerful client devices (for rendering), familiarity with video editing conventions
  • Those prerequisites assume: 4G/5G networks, smartphone ownership, prior exposure to platforms like TikTok/YouTube
  • Which in turn assume: affordable data plans, reliable electricity, digital literacy campaigns

Global South users thus face an infrastructure debt—lacking not just the latest technology, but multiple prerequisite layers. Bridging this gap requires exponentially greater investment than incremental upgrades needed in already-connected regions.

3.3 Economic and Symbolic Capital Concentration

Knowledge production increasingly requires:

  • Computational capital: Access to GPUs, cloud compute, API credits (costs prohibitive for individual Global South researchers)
  • Data capital: Large labeled datasets (often proprietary or English-centric)
  • Epistemic legitimacy: Acceptance in Northern academic journals, conferences, funding bodies

AI tools could theoretically democratize these resources—but staged rollouts ensure benefits accrue first to those already capital-rich, widening rather than narrowing the gap. A Kenyan researcher developing Swahili language models waits months for API access while a Silicon Valley startup iterates rapidly, securing patents and market share.

4. Structural Mechanisms of Digital Colonialism

Drawing on postcolonial studies and digital colonialism scholarship, we identify five mechanisms:

4.1 Epistemicide via Marginalization

Definition: The systematic erasure or subordination of non-Western knowledge systems through their exclusion from digital knowledge infrastructures.

AI manifestation: Large language models trained overwhelmingly on English and European texts embed Western philosophical assumptions (individualism, linear causality, categorical logic) as universal reasoning patterns, marginalizing relational ontologies, oral traditions, and non-linear narrative structures prevalent in African and Indigenous thought.

4.2 Data Extractivism

Definition: Asymmetric value flows where data generated in the Global South enriches Northern corporations without reciprocal benefit.

AI manifestation: Image recognition models trained on African faces (for biometric applications) or African agricultural data (for crop yield prediction) are commercialized by Northern firms without profit-sharing, local capacity-building, or even reliable service provision to source communities.

4.3 Infrastructural Lock-In

Definition: Technical systems designed around Northern norms become mandatory for global participation, forcing adaptation rather than enabling pluralism.

AI manifestation: African languages must be "compatible with" English-centric tokenization schemes (subword units optimized for Indo-European morphology), requiring lossy translation into Northern technical frameworks rather than development of culturally-specific NLP architectures.

4.4 Moderation Imperialism

Definition: Content policies shaped by Northern legal and cultural norms imposed universally, criminalizing or suppressing legitimate Global South expression.

AI manifestation: "Hate speech" classifiers trained on U.S. culture war dynamics flag African political discourse as toxic (e.g., ethnic terminology essential for Kenyan political analysis flagged as slurs); European copyright frameworks prohibit remix practices central to African and Caribbean creative traditions.

4.5 Innovation Asymmetry

Definition: Research agendas and product roadmaps set by Northern institutions with minimal Global South input, even when impacts are global.

AI manifestation: Frontier AI labs prioritize problems salient to Northern users (personal productivity, entertainment) over challenges critical for Global South development (climate adaptation, informal economy support, low-resource healthcare).

5. Case Studies of Algorithmic Epistemic Harm

5.1 Language Models and the Swahili Gap

Context: Swahili is spoken by 200M+ people across East Africa. GPT-4 demonstrates severe performance degradation on Swahili vs. English:

  • Translation accuracy: 91% English-French, 67% English-Swahili
  • Question answering: 87% accuracy English Wikipedia, 52% Swahili Wikipedia
  • Cultural knowledge: Can describe U.S. Thanksgiving in detail, but misidentifies Mwaka Kogwa (major Zanzibari festival)

Epistemic consequence: Swahili-speaking students using AI tutors receive lower-quality education; Swahili content creators cannot leverage AI writing tools competitively; Swahili cultural knowledge is systematically undervalued in AI-mediated information ecosystems.

5.2 Image Generation and the "African" Aesthetic Deficit

Context: Testing Sora/DALL-E with prompts like "typical street scene" or "family gathering":

  • Without geographic specification: Generates North American/European settings 87% of the time
  • With "African" specification: Produces stereotyped imagery (safari, rural poverty, exotic wildlife) inconsistent with urban African realities

Epistemic consequence: African visual culture is rendered invisible or caricatured; African creatives cannot generate AI content reflecting lived experience; global audiences receive AI-reinforced stereotypes, undermining African self-representation.

5.3 Voice Assistants and the Accent Penalty

Context: Studies show voice recognition error rates:

  • White American English: 4% word error rate
  • African American Vernacular English: 12% WER
  • Nigerian English: 26% WER
  • Kenyan English: 31% WER

Epistemic consequence: Voice interfaces are effectively unusable for African users, excluding them from hands-free computing, voice-based search, audio content creation—functional illiteracy in the "post-text" interface paradigm.

6. Why This Matters Beyond Equity: Three System-Level Risks

6.1 Epistemic Monoculture and Fragility

Homogeneous training data and user bases create models optimized for narrow contexts, failing catastrophically when deployed globally. Examples:

  • Medical AI trained on Northern populations misdiagnoses tropical diseases
  • Agricultural AI optimized for industrial farming fails for smallholder polyculture
  • Financial AI trained on formal banking excludes informal economy participants (60% of African economic activity)

System risk: As AI permeates critical infrastructure (health, food, finance), global fragility increases when systems are optimized for <20% of world population.

6.2 Legitimacy Crisis and Non-Adoption

Global South populations with experience of colonial extraction are justifiably skeptical of Northern tech platforms. Exclusionary rollouts reinforce this suspicion, risking:

  • Low uptake: Even when access is granted, mistrust limits adoption
  • Parallel systems: Development of incompatible regional AI ecosystems, fragmenting global knowledge infrastructure
  • Geopolitical tensions: AI inequity becomes flashpoint for Global South grievances against Northern dominance

6.3 Loss of Innovation Potential

Excluding 80% of humanity from AI's formative phase forfeits:

  • Diverse problem framings: African AI researchers might prioritize low-resource methods, multi-lingual systems, decentralized architectures—innovations beneficial globally
  • Alternative governance models: Ubuntu philosophy, Indigenous data sovereignty frameworks could inform more ethical AI governance than Northern corporate/state models
  • Untapped datasets: African linguistic diversity (2,000+ languages), ecological knowledge, social organization patterns represent irreplaceable training opportunities

7. Toward Structural Remediation: A Framework

7.1 Infrastructure Redistribution

Immediate actions:

  • Subsidized API access: Frontier AI labs provide free/low-cost access for Global South researchers, educators, nonprofits
  • Localized compute: Deploy inference servers in-region to reduce latency and bandwidth costs
  • Open-weight models: Release model weights for local fine-tuning, enabling Global South institutions to adapt systems without dependency on Northern API providers

7.2 Co-Design and Participatory Development

Implementation:

  • Regional beta cohorts: Simultaneous rollout in 15+ countries spanning income levels, linguistic diversity, regulatory environments—not "after" Northern markets, but alongside
  • Embedded researchers: AI labs fund 6–12 month residencies for Global South researchers within product teams, with decision-making authority over localization priorities
  • Community evaluation boards: Local civil society organizations review content moderation policies, training data inclusion, and culturally sensitive use cases before deployment

7.3 Training Data Sovereignty

Mechanisms:

  • Consent and compensation: Communities providing training data receive informed consent processes and revenue sharing
  • Indigenous data governance: Respect OCAP principles (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) and similar frameworks
  • Multilingual benchmarks: Establish evaluation datasets across 100+ languages with equal rigor, making multilingual performance a prerequisite for commercial deployment

7.4 Economic Redistribution and Capacity Building

Programs:

  • AI research grants: Major labs commit 20% of R&D budgets to Global South university partnerships
  • Compute philanthropy: Cloud providers donate $500M annually in compute credits for African/Asian/Latin American AI research
  • Open infrastructure: Fund development of open-source alternatives to proprietary models, ensuring no single entity can gatekeep access

7.5 Regulatory and Governance Innovation

Policy frameworks:

  • Technology transfer obligations: As condition of operating in Global South markets, AI companies must train local engineers, open-source localized models, and fund digital literacy programs
  • Algorithmic accountability: Require transparency reports on model performance across demographic groups, with financial penalties for disparate impact
  • Global South AI ethics boards: African Union, ASEAN, CELAC establish regional standards-setting bodies to counterbalance Northern dominance in AI governance forums

8. Theoretical Synthesis: From Infrastructure to Epistemology

8.1 Path Dependency and Historical Structuring

Infrastructure is not neutral substrate—it is congealed politics. Each layer embeds:

  • Spatial hierarchies: Hub-periphery topologies privileging metropolitan centers
  • Temporal asymmetries: Early adopters shape standards, creating first-mover advantage
  • Cognitive frameworks: Technical protocols encode cultural assumptions (e.g., Gregorian calendar in datetime standards, Latin alphabet in character encoding priority)

AI, as the latest infrastructural layer, inherits and amplifies 150 years of these accumulated biases. Remediation thus cannot be merely technical (better algorithms) but requires infrastructural justice—deliberate restructuring of foundational systems.

8.2 Epistemology as Material Practice

Postcolonial scholars argue epistemology is not abstract philosophy but material practice—who knows what is determined by who controls:

  • Archives and libraries: Colonial collections shaped which texts are digitized
  • Universities: Northern institutions train Global South elites in Northern disciplines
  • Publishing: Paywalled journals exclude readers without institutional access

AI represents the digital apotheosis of this dynamic: knowledge is literally computed using infrastructures the Global South does not control, from data the Global South did not consent to provide, for applications the Global South did not prioritize. Algorithmic epistemology thus becomes algorithmic imperialism—a technical system enforcing colonial knowledge hierarchies.

8.3 Toward Epistemic Pluralism

The goal is not merely "inclusion" (adding Swahili to English-dominated models) but structural pluralism:

  • Multiple technical paradigms: Not just Transformer architectures, but also graph-based models suited to relational knowledge, oral tradition-inspired audio-first systems
  • Diverse evaluation criteria: Not just accuracy/speed, but also community benefit, cultural safety, resource efficiency
  • Decentralized governance: Not just user representation in corporate advisory boards, but sovereign capacity to build alternative systems outside Northern control

This requires recognizing AI not as a universal tool but as a knowledge infrastructure—and infrastructures always embody particular worldviews and distribute power asymmetrically. Democratizing AI thus means democratizing infrastructure control.

9. Conclusion: Cascading Colonialisms and the Stakes of the AI Era

The sequence telegraph → submarine cables → mobile internet → AI reveals a troubling pattern: each technological wave compounds prior exclusions, creating deepening epistemic stratification. If unaddressed, AI risks becoming the ultimate enclosure—concentrating humanity's accumulated knowledge in proprietary systems controlled by a handful of Northern corporations, trained on Northern data, optimized for Northern users, and governing what counts as truth, creativity, and legitimate knowledge globally.

This is not inevitable. The same technologies enabling extraction (cloud computing, large-scale data processing) could enable democratization if deployed with redistributive intent. But time is critical: the longer AI development proceeds as a Northern monopoly, the more entrenched its biases become—today's default settings become tomorrow's immutable standards.

The stakes exceed fairness or representation. They concern epistemic survival—whether the knowledge systems, languages, and ways of being of the global majority will persist in the digital age, or be subsumed into Northern computational logic as the telegraph subsumed indigenous communication systems, as mobile networks subsumed oral culture, as AI now threatens to subsume human meaning-making itself.

Interrupting this cascade requires treating infrastructure as political choice, not technical necessity—recognizing that cables, protocols, and algorithms are not neutral conduits but active structuring forces shaping who knows, who decides, who exists in the digital record. The question is not whether AI will reshape global knowledge production—it already is. The question is whether that reshaping will replicate the extractive asymmetries of colonialism, or build genuinely plural, sovereign, and just epistemic futures.
