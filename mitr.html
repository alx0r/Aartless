MITRE: The Cybernetic War Machine

How control systems designed for tracking bombers became the infrastructure of permanent surveillance, and what this reveals about power, automation, and the erasure of democratic deliberation in the age of technical governance.

The Invisible Infrastructure

This analysis traces how cybernetics—the science of control and communication—evolved from WWII anti-aircraft predictors to become the foundational logic of modern governance, surveillance, and warfare. At the center of this story is MITRE Corporation, a "non-profit" that operates as permanent infrastructure for the national security state.

Core Thesis: MITRE represents the institutionalization of cybernetic control—from building systems that track bombers to building systems that track human behavior, always under the guise of technical neutrality while expanding military power into civilian life.

What MITRE Actually Is

Official Story

A trusted, independent, non-profit research center serving the public interest in national security and civilian infrastructure.

Reality

A permanent government contractor that operates as infrastructure—too embedded to fail, too classified to scrutinize, too "non-profit" to question.

Key Fact: MITRE is a Federally Funded Research and Development Center (FFRDC). This means no competitive bidding, permanent funding, and a mandate that survives every administration. They've been at it since 1958.

Origins: The Cybernetics Revolution (1940-1953)

Wiener's War Work: The Original Cybernetic Weapon

Before MITRE, before the conferences, before the theory, there was a practical problem that birthed cybernetics: How do you shoot down a fast-moving airplane?

The Predictor Problem (1940-1942)

  • German bombers move faster than human reflexes allow
  • By the time you see where the plane is, it's already somewhere else
  • Anti-aircraft guns must fire where the plane will be, not where it is
  • Solution: Predict the future from past behavior

Wiener's AA Predictor (1940-1943)

Radar tracks plane position over time
↓
Mathematical model extrapolates trajectory
↓
Servomechanism (automated control system) aims gun
↓
Gun fires at predicted future position
↓
System observes result (hit/miss)
↓
Adjusts model parameters (LEARNING)
↓
Next target engaged with improved prediction

This wasn't just a better gun—it was a new ontology:

  • The pilot becomes information: Not a human being, but a pattern of coordinates changing over time
  • The future becomes calculable: Statistical prediction replaces human judgment
  • The machine becomes autonomous: Servomechanisms adjust faster than human operators can intervene
  • Error becomes signal: Mistakes aren't failures—they're data for improving the model
Wiener's horror: He realized he'd created something profound and terrifying. In Cybernetics (1948) and The Human Use of Human Beings (1950), he warned that treating people as information flows—as targets to be predicted and controlled—would spread far beyond warfare. He was right, and ignored.

The Macy Conferences (1946-1953): Formalizing the Control Paradigm

The war work needed theory. In a Manhattan apartment, mathematicians, neurologists, anthropologists, and military strategists gathered to solve: How do you control complex systems?

Key Figures

  • Norbert Wiener (MIT): Coined "cybernetics" from the Greek for "steersman"—the art of control through feedback
  • John von Neumann: Computer architecture and game theory for nuclear strategy
  • Claude Shannon (MIT): Information theory—the mathematics of messages and signals
  • Warren McCulloch & Walter Pitts: Neurons as logic gates—brains are computers, computers are brains
  • Gregory Bateson: Showed how feedback loops govern families, cultures, ecosystems
The Revelation: Anti-aircraft guns, thermostats, nervous systems, economies, and societies all operate on the same principle—detect deviation from goal state, send corrective signal, measure effect, repeat. This is the cybernetic loop, and it works on anything.

MIT: The Forge of Cybernetic Warfare (1940-1960)

MIT wasn't just hosting conferences—it was building the infrastructure of cybernetic warfare:

Radiation Laboratory (1940-1945)

Developed microwave radar—the sensing apparatus for cybernetic systems. 4,000 people, $1.5 billion (inflation-adjusted), 100+ companies involved.

Servomechanisms Lab

Where Wiener worked on automated fire-control systems—guns that aimed themselves by predicting target trajectories.

Lincoln Laboratory (1951)

Created specifically to build SAGE—the first attempt to make an entire continent into a cybernetic system.

Critical Point: The Pentagon didn't just fund this research—it structured the entire epistemology. Problems were defined by military needs. Success meant operational deployment. "Dual-use" meant war logic embedded in civilian systems from the start.

Project Charles (1951): The Blueprint for Permanent Emergency

In summer 1951, MIT convened "Project Charles"—a secret study on how to defend America from Soviet bombers.

The Conclusion

America needed a computerized air defense system operating 24/7/365, processing data from hundreds of radars, automatically tracking thousands of targets, coordinating fighters and missiles—SAGE.

But more importantly: This required a permanent organization that could:

  • Operate outside normal government bureaucracy
  • Maintain security clearances for thousands of personnel
  • Bridge military needs and technical innovation
  • Survive changes in administration
  • Expand to new threats as technology evolved
The Birth of MITRE (1958): Spun out of MIT's Lincoln Lab to manage SAGE, MITRE was the organizational form cybernetic warfare required. Not quite government, not quite corporate, not quite academic—a new entity for a new kind of permanent war.

SAGE: The Prototype for Everything After (1958-1984)

SAGE (Semi-Automatic Ground Environment) was the first continent-scale computerized air defense system. This wasn't just a weapon system—it was a proof of concept that you could automate sovereignty itself.

23 radar sites across North America
↓
Data transmitted to AN/FSQ-7 computers (largest ever built)
↓
Automated threat assessment and track prediction
↓
Fighter jets and missiles receive targeting coordinates
↓
Human operators reduced to "exception handlers"
↓
System learns from every engagement

SAGE Established the Template

  • Total information awareness: Every inch of airspace monitored continuously
  • Machine speed decision-making: Humans too slow for jet-age war
  • Network architecture: Distributed sensing, centralized processing, distributed response
  • Predictive modeling: Don't just track threats—anticipate where they'll be
  • Permanent operation: The system never sleeps, because threats never stop
The Hidden Revolution: SAGE normalized the idea that automated systems should make life-and-death decisions faster than democratic deliberation allows. This is the original sin of cybernetic governance.

Derrida's Trace: The Program as Metaphysics

Of Grammatology and the Cybernetic Age

When Jacques Derrida wrote Of Grammatology (1967), he was watching cybernetics colonize everything. His intervention was radical: cybernetics doesn't just describe reality—it reveals the truth that Western metaphysics has always denied.

"If the theory of cybernetics is by itself to oust all metaphysical concepts—including the concepts of soul, of life, of value, of choice, of memory—which until recently served to separate the machine from man, it must conserve the notion of writing, trace, written mark, or grapheme."

What Does This Mean?

The West has always privileged:

  • Speech over writing: Speaking is direct, present, authentic; writing is secondary, derivative, a mere representation
  • Presence over absence: What is immediately given to consciousness is real; mediation is suspect
  • Meaning over signal: Content matters, form is just the container
  • Human over machine: We have consciousness, intentionality, free will; machines merely execute

Cybernetics inverts all of this—and Derrida says: good, because it was always backwards.

The Program: Writing Before Speech

Consider what MITRE does:

  • Threat models are written before threats appear
  • CVE identifiers are assigned before anyone understands the vulnerability
  • The ATT&CK framework defines adversaries before observing their behavior
  • System architectures are specified before systems exist

This is the program—not a plan you write down, but the logic of inscription that precedes and enables all execution.

Traditional metaphysics:
INTENTION (in the mind) → SPEECH (direct expression) → WRITING (recording)

Cybernetic reality:
PROGRAM (written protocol) → EXECUTION (automated) → "INTENTION" (retroactive attribution)
Derrida's insight: The second has always been true. We just pretended otherwise.

Dual-Use: War Logic in Civilian Clothes

The same systems that track missiles track migrants. The same algorithms that target combatants target content moderation. This isn't accidental—it's infrastructural.

Examples of Cybernetic Colonization

  • Air traffic control: Built from bomber tracking—same sensors, same prediction models, same command-and-control logic
  • Border surveillance: Adapted from battlefield awareness systems—predict crossings, preempt movement, optimize interdiction
  • Critical infrastructure protection: Originally nuclear facility security—now manages power grids, water systems, with military response protocols
  • Cyber threat intelligence: NSA tradecraft packaged for corporate security—same attribution methods, same offensive/defensive ambiguity
There is no "civilian" cybernetics. Systems designed for war carry war logic into everyday life. Efficiency becomes kill-chain speed. Optimization becomes casualty minimization. Safety becomes threat elimination.

"Warfighter-Centric" Acquisition: The Closure Tightens

MITRE's September 2025 paper represents a sophisticated evolution in military-technical control discourse. What appears as "empowerment" is actually the perfection of cybernetic integration—closing the loop between human and machine, developer and operator, policy and execution.

The Problem Framing: Human as System Bottleneck

MITRE identifies a "pathology" in current acquisition:

  • Waterfall development creates "dangerous" systems: Patriot friendly fire incidents, USS Vincennes shooting down a civilian airliner—because human feedback comes too late in the development cycle
  • The gap between designer and operator: Engineers build what they think warfighters need, discover problems only after deployment
  • Multi-year acquisition cycles: By the time a system is fielded, the battlefield has changed

Proposed solution: Continuous warfighter feedback loops to eliminate system latency.

Control logic: This isn't about empowering humans—it's about optimizing human-machine integration for lethal efficiency. The "bottleneck" isn't bureaucracy; it's the last remaining gap between sensing and shooting.

The Ukrainian Model: Weaponized Agile as Battlefield Reality

MITRE points to Ukraine's drone warfare as the template:

  • Drone workshops embedded in combat brigades: 10-12 soldier-technicians modifying systems in real-time based on battlefield performance
  • Constant iteration: Firmware updates, hardware modifications, tactical adjustments happening at the pace of combat
  • No distinction between R&D and operations: The workshop IS the battlefield, the battlefield IS the lab

MITRE's vision: Scale this model across DoD—embed the R&D pipeline directly into combat units.

Control implication: Eliminates the distinction between developer and operator, creating seamless kill chain integration. Every warfighter becomes a sensor node providing feedback for system optimization. This is the cybernetic loop perfected—the human becomes part of the machine's learning algorithm.

The "Warfighter-Centric" Paradox

This paper reveals the fundamental contradiction in modern military technocracy: the rhetoric of human-centered design serves to more deeply embed humans within technical control systems.

The "warfighter" becomes the ultimate sensor and optimization mechanism for a kill chain that increasingly operates beyond human timescales and comprehension. What appears as democratization is actually the perfecting of control—creating a military organism where every soldier becomes both sensor and actuator in a globally integrated combat system.

The ATT&CK Framework: Ontology as Infrastructure

MITRE's ATT&CK framework deserves special attention because it exemplifies how technical infrastructure becomes epistemic infrastructure—how tools for organizing knowledge shape what can be known.

What ATT&CK Does

ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) is a knowledge base of cyber adversary behavior based on real-world observations. It catalogs:

  • Tactics: The adversary's technical goals (initial access, persistence, privilege escalation, etc.)
  • Techniques: How adversaries achieve each tactical goal (specific methods and procedures)
  • Sub-techniques: Variations and specific implementations
  • Procedures: Specific instances used by identified threat groups

Why This Matters

ATT&CK has become the global standard for discussing cyber threats. Security vendors build products around ATT&CK. Government agencies require ATT&CK compliance. Threat intelligence reports map findings to ATT&CK. Red teams structure assessments using ATT&CK. Purple teams coordinate using ATT&CK.

The Consequence: MITRE doesn't just document threats—it structures how threats can be perceived, discussed, and addressed. Attacks that don't fit the framework become invisible. Defenses that don't map to ATT&CK appear incomplete. Alternative taxonomies compete at a profound disadvantage because they lack ATT&CK's network effects and institutional backing.

The Hidden Politics

  • What gets categorized determines what gets defended against: Prioritizing nation-state adversaries over cybercriminal networks shapes security investment
  • How techniques are grouped determines how defenses are organized: The taxonomy structures institutional response
  • Which threat actors get named and tracked determines geopolitical narratives: Attribution shapes international relations
  • What counts as "common knowledge" determines what remains secret: The boundary between ATT&CK and classified intelligence is itself a political choice

The Epistemological Problem: Who Knows What MITRE Knows?

MITRE occupies a uniquely privileged position in the knowledge architecture of the national security state:

Information Asymmetry

  • MITRE sees across agencies: Works with DoD, DHS, FAA, Intelligence Community simultaneously—understands how systems interconnect in ways no single agency does
  • MITRE sees classified and unclassified: Bridges secret and public worlds, knowing what vulnerabilities are exploited in ways that public researchers cannot
  • MITRE defines what counts as knowledge: Through CVE, ATT&CK, and other taxonomies, MITRE structures how threats are understood and discussed
  • MITRE controls access to its own knowledge: Can classify research findings, withhold threat intelligence, restrict access to methodologies

The Oversight Paradox

How do you oversee an organization whose expertise is precisely what makes oversight necessary?

  • Congressional committees lack the technical knowledge to evaluate MITRE's claims
  • Independent auditors lack security clearances
  • Academic researchers lack access to operational data
  • Journalists face classification barriers and national security justifications for opacity
The technical is political when technical decisions determine political outcomes without political deliberation. MITRE makes choices about system architecture, threat prioritization, and resource allocation that shape who gets surveilled, who gets secured, and whose security matters.

The Question of Complicity

Engineers at MITRE often see themselves as simply solving technical problems. They are improving air traffic safety, strengthening cybersecurity defenses, making government systems more efficient. They are not soldiers; they don't pull triggers. Many genuinely believe they are serving the public interest.

The Problem of Compartmentalization

MITRE's classification systems and need-to-know restrictions mean that no individual engineer sees the full picture:

  • The radar engineer doesn't see the deportation statistics
  • The algorithm developer doesn't see the targeting decisions

Distributed Responsibility: When harm results from complex systems, no individual feels responsible. Everyone was "just doing their job," following protocols, meeting specifications, solving technical problems. The harm emerges from the system architecture itself, which no one person designed or controls.

Alternatives and Resistances: What Could Be Different?

Critique without alternatives is incomplete. What would it mean to organize security infrastructure differently?

Democratic Oversight Models

  • Public FFRDC board: Representatives from civil liberties organizations, academic institutions, and affected communities with voting power over project authorization
  • Mandatory transparency timelines: All non-operationally-sensitive research findings published within 2 years
  • Adversarial auditing: Independent teams with equivalent clearances and access empowered to challenge MITRE's conclusions
  • Rotating staff: Prevent institutional capture by limiting tenure and requiring circulation between MITRE, government, academia, and civil society

Alternative Epistemic Frameworks

  • Security as public health: Rather than adversarial threat models, conceptualize security as ecosystem resilience
  • Vulnerability as systemic property: Focus on reducing attack surface through simplification rather than layering countermeasures
  • Distributed authority: Multiple independent organizations maintaining competing frameworks rather than a single obligatory standard
  • Open source intelligence: Prioritize publicly auditable methods over classified capabilities

Practical Interventions

  • FOIA requests: Systematic campaigns to force disclosure of MITRE contracts, methodologies, and findings
  • Alternative taxonomies: Develop and promote threat intelligence frameworks that embed different values and priorities
  • Critical technical practice: Engineers and researchers who work within these systems while maintaining critical distance
  • Public scholarship: Making these dynamics visible and comprehensible beyond specialist communities
The Challenge: These alternatives face the fundamental problem that security infrastructure exhibits extreme network effects. The more widely adopted a standard becomes, the more valuable it is to adopt, creating self-reinforcing dominance. Displacing incumbent infrastructure requires coordinated action at a scale that the incumbent actively works to prevent.

Conclusion: Living in the Cybernetic State

MITRE represents the maturation of cybernetic governance—the moment when control systems become so comprehensive, so embedded, so naturalized that alternatives become difficult to imagine. This is not because of any conspiracy or deliberate malevolence, but because of how infrastructure works: it becomes invisible, assumed, "just the way things are."

What We Know

  • SAGE was the prototype for continent-scale automated control
  • MITRE was created to operationalize this vision permanently
  • Cybernetics provides the logic: detect, predict, respond, optimize
  • Military origins shape civilian applications through dual-use infrastructure
  • Technical decisions become political outcomes without democratic deliberation
  • Emergency justifications produce permanent expansions of control
  • Expertise becomes authority, and authority evades accountability

What Remains to Be Done

Understanding MITRE is necessary but insufficient. The challenge is developing practices of resistance that don't simply demand transparency (which the system can absorb) or regulation (which the system can capture) but that fundamentally question whether security should be organized through cybernetic control at all.

This requires:

  • Denaturalization: Making visible the contingent choices embedded in systems that appear inevitable
  • Alternative epistemologies: Developing ways of knowing security that don't default to prediction and control
  • Democratic experimentalism: Creating spaces where technical decisions are subject to genuine political deliberation
  • Infrastructural imagination: Refusing the claim that current systems represent the only possible arrangements
The question is not whether we can return to a pre-cybernetic state—we cannot. The question is whether cybernetic systems will continue to be organized around military logic, or whether they can be reconstructed according to different values: care rather than control, resilience rather than domination, transparency rather than classification, democracy rather than technocracy.

Final Reflection

MITRE began by tracking Soviet bombers. Today it tracks migrants, manages healthcare records, defines cybersecurity standards, coordinates air traffic, and influences virtually every domain of American infrastructure. The threat changed, but the infrastructure persisted—and grew.

This is the cybernetic war machine: not a conspiracy, but a system architecture that perpetually produces the necessity of its own expansion. Wiener saw it coming in 1950. Derrida analyzed its metaphysical implications in 1967. Foucault mapped its operations throughout the 1970s. Agamben warned about the permanent state of exception in 2005.

The warnings were not heeded because the infrastructure was already built. Now the question is whether we can build differently, or whether the cybernetic state represents the permanent condition of technological modernity.

MITRE is not the problem—MITRE is the symptom. The problem is a political order that has outsourced sovereignty to technical systems that operate beyond democratic accountability, justified by emergencies that never end.
