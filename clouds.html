Environmental Media & Control Societies

A framework for understanding digital infrastructure as governance apparatus

Part 1: Peters on Infrastructure as Control

"Digital media reactivate not only these old limbic fluids, but older forms of data use. Unlike the mass media of the twentieth century, digital media traffic less in content, programs, and opinions than in organization, power, and calculation. Digital media serve more as logistical devices of tracking and orientation than in providing unifying stories to the society at large. Digital media revive ancient navigational functions: they point us in time and space, index our data, and keep us on the grid."
— John Durham Peters, The Marvelous Clouds (2015)
  • Environmental media = control apparatus: Infrastructure itself governs, not just the content it carries. The grid—satellites, cables, APIs, protocols—is the primary mechanism of power in what Deleuze called "societies of control," where regulation happens through continuous modulation rather than disciplinary confinement.
  • Body as data substrate: Peters' "limbic fluids" refer to pre-cognitive, evolutionary aspects of embodied communication. In algorithmic governance, these become biometric capture points—facial expressions, gait patterns, voice cadence—that enable surveillance below the threshold of conscious resistance.
  • Logistical colonialism: Writing's first purpose wasn't literature but Mesopotamian inventory management (tracking beer, wheat, labor). Digital media scale this ancient logic through AI and metadata: populations, resources, and behaviors are tracked not through ideological narratives but through infrastructural accounting.
  • Epistemic enclosure: Digital platforms collapse public knowledge production into proprietary databases. Power operates through infrastructure ownership (who controls the servers, the APIs, the training data) rather than overt content censorship—a technological aristocracy built on architectural gatekeeping.
  • Social media = modulated control: These platforms don't eliminate the risks of face-to-face interaction; they replace bodily unpredictability with algorithmic mediation. Instead of disciplinary institutions that confine, we get continuous micro-adjustments through feeds, notifications, and recommendation systems.

Part 2: Resistance Strategies

  • Federated architectures: Protocols like ActivityPub (Mastodon) and Matrix distribute infrastructure ownership across multiple servers, preventing any single entity from monopolizing the epistemic commons. Users can migrate between instances while maintaining social connections, breaking platform lock-in.
  • End-to-end encryption as default: Tools like Signal and SimpleX eliminate metadata capture points before surveillance systems can process them. E2EE disrupts logistical tracking by ensuring only endpoints (not intermediaries) can read communications.
  • Open-source everything: Transparency in code creates auditable power structures. Proprietary "black boxes" enable unaccountable governance because their operations remain hidden. Contributing to and funding FOSS projects (Linux, Firefox, LibreOffice) builds collective infrastructure.
  • Data unions and cooperatives: Platform cooperativism enables collective bargaining over biometric and behavioral data. Rather than individual consent theater, organized groups negotiate terms—examples include driver cooperatives challenging Uber/Lyft and Resonate's artist-owned music streaming.
  • Mesh networks: Community-owned broadband (NYC Mesh) and LoRa networks create physical infrastructure autonomy, bypassing ISP chokepoints. Distributed routing makes centralized shutdowns or throttling exponentially harder.
  • Cryptographic commons: IPFS, Tor, and I2P provide decentralized storage and routing. Content lives across distributed nodes rather than corporate servers, making seizure or tracking difficult. Hosting exit nodes and seeding archives are direct mutual aid practices.
  • Refuse biometric capture: Opt out of facial recognition systems, voice assistants, and fitness trackers wherever possible. Starving the "limbic fluid" data pipeline at its input stage reduces the raw material available for algorithmic profiling.
  • Support right-to-repair: Hardware longevity reduces vendor lock-in and the forced migration onto increasingly surveilled upgrade cycles. Fighting planned obsolescence is both ecological and anti-surveillance praxis.
  • Algorithmic literacy campaigns: Understanding how recommendation systems, engagement metrics, and A/B testing modulate behavior is prerequisite to resisting them. Teach the grid's operations to make conscious refusal possible.

Part 3: Related Disciplinary Fields

  • Science & Technology Studies (STS): Bruno Latour's actor-network theory demonstrates that non-human entities—algorithms, cables, satellites—possess political agency. Infrastructure isn't a neutral container for governance; it is governance. Technical objects actively shape social relations and power distributions.
  • Platform Studies: Bogost and Montfort's framework examines how hardware and software constraints constitute political architecture. API rate limits, chipset dependencies, and OS privileges determine what actions are possible. Deleuze's control societies operate precisely through this platform-level modulation: continuous micro-adjustments replace the discrete enclosures (prisons, schools, factories) of disciplinary power.
  • Postcolonial Computing: Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias theorize "data colonialism"—the application of extractive colonial logic to behavior and attention. The Global South frequently serves as testing ground for surveillance technologies (India's Aadhaar biometric system, Kenya's M-Pesa financial tracking) before metropolitan deployment. Asymmetric connectivity produces asymmetric vulnerability: populations with less infrastructural power face greater exposure to experimental control systems.
