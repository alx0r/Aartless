The Deep Short

An Investigation into the AI Bubble, Capital-Efficient Disruption, and the New States of Control
By the w0lfed• Special Dispatch from the Digital Frontier
The trading floor in an earlier era—a scene of human calculation soon to be superseded by algorithmic intelligence

In the waning months of 2025, a seismic tremor has fractured the foundations of the artificial intelligence industry. What began as whispered skepticism in trading floors has erupted into a full-scale market reckoning. The so-called "AI Bubble"—inflated by years of hyperbolic promises and billion-dollar valuations—now faces its moment of truth.

At the center of this maelstrom stands a paradox: the very sanctions designed to throttle China's technological ascent have instead catalyzed a revolution in efficiency. Chinese laboratories, operating under severe constraints, have pioneered models that achieve state-of-the-art performance for a fraction—a mere pittance—of what American giants spend.

The market has taken notice. Legendary investor Michael Burry, famed for anticipating the 2008 financial collapse, has placed a staggering $1.1 billion wager against the industry's titans. His target: NVIDIA and Palantir, the very companies Wall Street had crowned as untouchable.

This is not merely a story of market speculation. It is a tale of geopolitical chess, technological disruption, and the fundamental question of who will control the infrastructure of intelligence in the 21st century.

Part I: The New Contenders

§ 1. Moonshot AI's Kimi K2 Thinking

The dominant narrative of 2023 and 2024 held a simple truth: leadership in artificial intelligence was a function of capital expenditure. Build bigger. Spend more. The companies with the deepest pockets would win. This "brute force" paradigm created an impenetrable moat around a handful of American hyperscalers—OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta.

Then came November 2025, and everything changed.

Moonshot AI, a relatively obscure startup backed by Alibaba, released Kimi K2 Thinking. The model's specifications read like a direct refutation of everything Silicon Valley believed about AI development. One trillion total parameters. Only 32 billion activated per inference. A 256,000-token context window—enough to process entire books in a single pass.

But the most explosive detail was buried in the technical documentation: training cost of $4.6 million.

To understand the gravity of this figure, consider the landscape it disrupted. OpenAI's GPT-4, released in 2023, cost an estimated $79 million to train. Google's Gemini Ultra: $192 million. Meta's Llama 3.1-405B: $170 million. Industry analysts projected GPT-5's training budget would exceed $1 billion, possibly reaching $1.8 billion when accounting for infrastructure and talent costs.

Kimi K2 Thinking achieved comparable—and in some cases superior—performance for less than 0.5% of these costs.

§ 2. Performance Verification: The Benchmark Wars

Skeptics immediately questioned whether a model trained so cheaply could truly compete with its expensive rivals. The answer came in the form of standardized benchmarks—tests designed to be immune to gaming or memorization.

On Humanity's Last Exam (HLE), a "Google-proof" evaluation of expert-level reasoning, Kimi K2 Thinking achieved 44.9% accuracy. This surpassed OpenAI's GPT-5 (41.7%) and Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.5 (32.0%).

On BrowseComp, which tests an AI's ability to autonomously navigate the web and locate obscure information, Kimi K2 scored 60.2%—more than double the human baseline of 29.2%.

On SWE-Bench Verified, the industry-standard coding challenge, the model achieved 71.3%, placing it among the elite tier.

These were not marginal improvements. These were decisive victories.

§ 3. The DeepSeek Anomaly

Before Kimi K2, there was DeepSeek. And before the market truly believed, there was January 2025.

DeepSeek AI is not a typical technology company. It is owned and operated by High-Flyer, a Chinese quantitative hedge fund. Its founder, Liang Wenfeng, serves as CEO of both entities. This unusual structure reveals the company's fundamental logic: DeepSeek exists not to maximize user growth or advertising revenue, but to identify and exploit market inefficiencies.

In January 2025, DeepSeek released its R1 model—a reasoning-focused AI trained for an astonishing $294,000 to $6 million (estimates vary). The model performed on par with OpenAI's then-proprietary reasoning model, which cost orders of magnitude more.

The market's reaction was immediate and violent. Within days, tech stocks hemorrhaged value. NVIDIA, the chipmaker whose GPUs powered the AI revolution, suffered the largest single-day market cap loss in corporate history: $600 billion erased in hours. The broader tech sector lost over $1 trillion in valuation.

Wall Street called it "AI's Sputnik moment." But unlike the 1957 satellite launch that galvanized American resolve, this shock paralyzed it. If a hedge fund's side project could match OpenAI's flagship product for pocket change, what justified the astronomical valuations?

⚠ The Radio Silence Myth ⚠

Western media reported that DeepSeek went "silent" after January's disruption, fueling speculation of regulatory crackdowns or technical failures. This narrative is provably false.

DeepSeek's GitHub repositories and API documentation show continuous development throughout 2025: DeepSeek-V2.5, V3.1-Think, V3.2-Exp, and enhanced Coder variants all shipped on schedule.

The "silence" was a Western perception problem, not a Chinese development problem. The company simply returned to building while markets panicked.

Part II: The Deep Short

§ 1. Michael Burry's Billion-Dollar Bet

Michael Burry is not a man prone to reckless speculation. The former physician turned hedge fund manager earned his reputation—and his fortune—by anticipating catastrophes others refused to see. In 2008, while Wall Street partied on subprime mortgages, Burry bet billions against the housing market. He was proven spectacularly correct.

Now, in late 2025, Burry has taken aim at artificial intelligence.

According to SEC filings from Q3 2025, Scion Asset Management holds put options with a notional value of $1.1 billion targeting two companies: NVIDIA ($187 million) and Palantir ($912 million). Put options are financial instruments that profit when stock prices fall—they are, quite literally, bets against success.

When news of the positions leaked, Palantir CEO Alex Karp dismissed Burry as "batshit." The stock dipped anyway.

But Burry's thesis is not emotional—it is coldly mathematical. The short position rests on a simple premise: proprietary AI companies are structurally overvalued because their moats have been commoditized.

§ 2. The Cost-Per-Capability Crisis

The fundamental problem facing companies like OpenAI and Anthropic is that their business models depend on an economic moat that no longer exists. They charge premium prices for API access—$15 per million input tokens for GPT-5 Pro, $75 per million output tokens for Claude Opus 4.1—because they assumed customers had no alternatives.

This assumption is now demonstrably false.

Kimi K2 Thinking and DeepSeek Coder V2 are available as open-weight models. Anyone can download them. Anyone can run them locally or deploy them on inexpensive cloud infrastructure. The marginal cost to users approaches zero.

For complex reasoning tasks, Claude Opus 4.1 can charge over $7 per query. Kimi K2 performs the same task for the cost of inference—pennies, if that.

Investors are beginning to ask the uncomfortable question: Why would anyone pay for a proprietary model when superior open-source alternatives exist?

Part III: Geopolitics & The Sanctions Paradox

§ 1. The Architecture of Constraint

In October 2022, the Biden administration enacted sweeping export controls on advanced semiconductor technology to China. The goal was explicit: prevent China from developing cutting-edge AI by denying access to the most powerful chips.

The restrictions were expanded in 2023 and again in 2024. By 2025, Chinese AI labs were operating under severe hardware constraints, forced to train models on older, less efficient GPUs like the NVIDIA A100 rather than the state-of-the-art H100 or H200.

US policymakers celebrated these sanctions as a strategic masterstroke. They were not.

Rather than capitulating, Chinese researchers adapted. Denied the ability to compete in raw computational power, they innovated in software architecture. They pioneered sparse activation techniques like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). They developed more efficient training algorithms. They optimized for inference speed and energy consumption.

The result: models that required less compute, less energy, and less capital—but performed just as well, if not better.

The sanctions had backfired. By forcing Chinese labs off the "brute force" treadmill, the US inadvertently accelerated the development of the very techniques now threatening American dominance.

⚙ The Sanctions Timeline ⚙
  • Oct 2022: Biden administration enacts chip export controls targeting AI accelerators
  • Oct 2023: Expanded restrictions on A100/H100 sales to China
  • Jan 2024: Additional controls on cloud computing access
  • Jan 2025: DeepSeek R1 proves Chinese labs achieved SOTA despite sanctions
  • Nov 2025: Kimi K2 demonstrates superiority of capital-efficient architecture

§ 2. Open-Source as Geopolitical Strategy

China's embrace of open-source AI is not altruism—it is strategy.

By releasing models like Kimi K2 and DeepSeek as open-weight projects, Chinese labs accomplish multiple goals simultaneously:

First: They undermine the proprietary business models of American competitors. Why pay OpenAI when an equivalent model is free?

Second: They accelerate global adoption. Open-source models integrate into products, services, and infrastructure worldwide, creating dependency on Chinese innovation.

Third: They bypass export controls. Software cannot be sanctioned as easily as hardware. An open-source model can proliferate anywhere, instantly.

Fourth: They demonstrate technological superiority. Releasing a model openly is an act of confidence—it says, "We are so far ahead, we can give this away for free."

This is soft power in the digital age: control not through restriction, but through proliferation.

Part IV: Critical Theory & States of Control

§ 1. Marxist Analysis: The Commodification Crisis

Karl Marx understood that capitalism contains its own contradictions. One such contradiction is the tendency toward overproduction—the creation of surplus commodities that outstrip demand, collapsing prices and profit margins.

The AI bubble represents a textbook case of this dynamic. American tech companies invested hundreds of billions into proprietary models, assuming they could extract monopoly rents indefinitely. But capital, Marx noted, always seeks to lower costs. The moment a cheaper substitute emerges, the overvalued commodity crashes.

Open-source AI is that substitute. It represents, in Marxist terms, the socialization of the means of production. The "means" here being intelligence itself—the algorithms, weights, and architectures that constitute AI. When these are made public, the private accumulation model collapses.

Chinese labs, operating under state direction and hedge fund pragmatism, have effectively hijacked the capitalist logic of competition to produce a post-capitalist outcome: free, universally accessible intelligence.

The irony is profound: sanctions designed to preserve American tech hegemony have accelerated its unraveling.

§ 2. Foucauldian Power-Knowledge

Michel Foucault argued that power and knowledge are inseparable—that those who control knowledge production control society. AI represents the ultimate power-knowledge nexus: the ability to process, interpret, and generate information at superhuman scale.

Under the proprietary model, this power is centralized. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google control access via APIs, usage policies, and pricing structures. They determine what questions can be asked, what outputs are permissible, what uses are forbidden. This is discipline through architecture—a digital panopticon where the model "sees" all user queries and the company retains ultimate control.

Open-source models disrupt this power structure. When the model is public, no central authority can gate access or enforce norms. Power diffuses. Knowledge proliferates.

But this is not pure liberation. Foucault would caution that power never disappears—it merely reconfigures. If Chinese models dominate the open-source ecosystem, does power shift to Beijing? Do new forms of control emerge through hardware dependencies, training data, or geopolitical leverage?

The struggle over AI is not just technological—it is a struggle over the architecture of power itself.

§ 3. Postcolonial Critique: Techno-Imperialism

Edward Said's concept of Orientalism—the West's tendency to define and dominate the "East" through knowledge production—finds new expression in the AI arms race. US sanctions against Chinese AI development rest on an assumption of technological superiority, a presumption that the West defines the frontier and others merely follow.

The DeepSeek and Kimi K2 disruptions shatter this narrative. They represent what postcolonial theorists call "decolonial innovation"—technological development that refuses Western paradigms and charts alternative paths.

China's capital-efficient approach is not just cheaper—it is philosophically distinct. Where Silicon Valley embraced maximalism (bigger models, more compute, higher costs), Chinese labs pursued minimalism (sparse activation, efficiency, accessibility). This reflects different values: not "grow at all costs" but "optimize within constraints."

The sanctions, viewed through this lens, are an attempt at technological colonialism—using state power to prevent the "periphery" from competing with the "core." But like all colonial projects, they have generated resistance. The result is not Chinese subservience but Chinese ascendancy.

"The sanctions did not stop China's AI development. They redirected it—and the redirection may prove more threatening than the original path."

§ 4. The New States of Control

Gilles Deleuze, writing in 1990, anticipated a shift from Foucault's "disciplinary societies" to what he called "societies of control." In control societies, power operates not through confinement (prisons, schools, factories) but through continuous modulation—credit scores, algorithms, data profiles that follow individuals everywhere.

AI represents the perfection of control society. It does not confine users; it shapes their informational environment. It does not prohibit; it recommends, suggests, autocompletes. Power becomes ambient, invisible, ubiquitous.

The battle between proprietary and open-source AI is thus a battle over the architecture of control:

Proprietary Control: Centralized, accountable to shareholders, subject to regulation but also to profit motive. Data sovereignty resides with corporations. Users are products.

Open-Source Control: Decentralized, unaccountable, resistant to regulation but also to capture. Data sovereignty diffuses. Users are autonomous—but so are bad actors.

Neither model is inherently liberatory. Both can oppress. The question is: Who decides the parameters of control, and how do we contest them?

Part VI: Market Analysis & Implications

§ 1. The Valuation Disconnect

Wall Street's AI valuations rest on several assumptions, all now in question:

Assumption 1: State-of-the-art AI requires massive capital investment, creating insurmountable barriers to entry.

Reality: Chinese labs have achieved SOTA for <1% of projected US costs.

Assumption 2: Proprietary models maintain performance advantages worth premium pricing.

Reality: Open-source models match or exceed proprietary performance on key benchmarks.

Assumption 3: API business models create durable moats through network effects and ecosystem lock-in.

Reality: When superior models are freely available, lock-in dissolves. Switching costs approach zero.

Assumption 4: US technological leadership in AI is secure and will compound over time.

Reality: Chinese labs are releasing more open-source models, faster, with better cost efficiency.

These assumption failures explain both the $1 trillion January crash and Michael Burry's billion-dollar short. The market is beginning to price in a new reality: the AI gold rush may be over, and those who spent billions prospecting may have little to show for it.

§ 2. NVIDIA's Dilemma

NVIDIA's predicament is particularly acute. The company's valuation exploded from $360 billion in early 2023 to over $3 trillion at its peak in 2024, making it briefly the world's most valuable company. This growth was predicated entirely on AI demand for its H100 and H200 GPUs.

But if models can be trained for $5-10 million instead of $500 million, and if sanctions prevent selling to China anyway, and if open-source models reduce the need for continuous expensive retraining—what happens to demand?

The January 2025 crash erased $600 billion from NVIDIA's market cap in a single day. By November 2025, the company had shed over $1 trillion from its peak. Investors are questioning whether the AI infrastructure boom is sustainable if the software layer has commoditized.

Burry's $187 million short position against NVIDIA is not a bet that GPUs are obsolete—it is a bet that the company is overvalued relative to a future where AI training costs plummet and Chinese alternatives proliferate.

§ 3. The Palantir Paradox

Palantir's inclusion in Burry's short thesis is more nuanced. The defense and intelligence contractor has positioned itself as an "AI-powered data platform," commanding premium pricing for its Foundry and Gotham products.

But if the underlying AI models powering these platforms can be replicated with open-source alternatives, what is Palantir actually selling? Integration services? Government relationships? The ability to deploy classified systems?

Burry's $912 million notional short suggests he believes the answer is "not enough to justify a $100+ billion valuation." CEO Alex Karp's dismissal of Burry as "batshit" reflects the cognitive dissonance afflicting much of the industry: the refusal to accept that the moat has been breached.

Part VII: Future Trajectories

§ 1. Scenario Analysis

Three plausible futures emerge from the current dynamics:

Scenario A: Open-Source Dominance

Chinese models continue improving and proliferating. Proprietary APIs become niche products for enterprises requiring compliance guarantees. US companies shift to services and integration rather than model development. NVIDIA pivots to inference hardware. The market corrects 40-60% from peak valuations.

Scenario B: Regulatory Backlash

US and EU governments impose restrictions on open-source AI deployment, citing national security and safety concerns. "AI licensing" requirements create artificial barriers. Chinese models are banned from critical infrastructure. A bifurcated ecosystem emerges: open-source for hobbyists, proprietary for enterprises. Incumbents stabilize but growth slows.

Scenario C: Technological Leapfrog

US labs achieve a genuine breakthrough—quantum-enhanced training, neuromorphic architectures, or AGI-level reasoning—that cannot be easily replicated. The capital-intensive model proves justified in hindsight. Chinese efficiency gains are revealed as incremental. Markets recover. The bubble continues inflating.

As of November 2025, Scenario A appears most likely, Scenario B is actively being pursued by policymakers, and Scenario C remains speculative.

§ 2. The 2030 Chinese AI Plan

China's State Council set a clear objective in 2017: achieve global leadership in AI by 2030. This goal, initially dismissed as aspirational, now appears achievable.

The strategy has three pillars:

1. Talent Development: National directives to train AI researchers, with universities producing 50,000+ AI-focused graduates annually.

2. Capital Efficiency: Focus on doing more with less, particularly crucial under sanctions. DeepSeek and Kimi K2 exemplify this approach.

3. Open Proliferation: Release models publicly to establish ecosystems, dependencies, and global adoption before competitors react.

If current trends continue, China will likely lead in: total number of AI models released, open-source adoption rates, AI hardware deployment in consumer devices, and practical AI applications in manufacturing and infrastructure.

The US may retain advantages in: theoretical research, defense applications, proprietary enterprise solutions, and chip manufacturing (for now).

But in the race that matters—who provides the AI that ordinary people actually use—China is winning.

§ 3. Governance & Ethics Gaps

The rapid commoditization of AI creates governance challenges that existing frameworks cannot address:

Safety: Open-source models cannot be "aligned" or "controlled" centrally. If a model enables harmful outputs, there is no company to sue or regulate. Responsibility diffuses to the point of invisibility.

Bias: Chinese models are trained predominantly on Chinese data, reflecting Chinese cultural norms and government priorities. As these models proliferate globally, whose values are embedded in the world's AI infrastructure?

Militarization: Open-source AI accelerates autonomous weapons development. Export controls on models are nearly impossible to enforce. The Geneva Conventions have no jurisdiction over GitHub.

Labor: As AI capabilities improve and costs plummet, automation accelerates. Creative workers, programmers, analysts—all face commodification of their cognitive labor. The social safety net is unprepared.

Truth: When AI-generated content becomes indistinguishable from human-created content, and when the AI is free and unrestricted, information ecosystems collapse. We are not ready for this.

"The question is not whether open-source AI will dominate. The question is whether civilization can adapt to a world where intelligence is abundant, free, and uncontrolled."

Part VIII: Conclusions & Recommendations

This investigation has documented a structural transformation in the global AI landscape. The key findings bear repeating:

1. Chinese AI labs have achieved state-of-the-art performance at 1-2% of US costs through architectural innovation forced by sanctions.

2. Open-source distribution has commoditized capabilities previously worth hundreds of billions in market valuation.

3. The "Deep Short"—Burry's $1.1B bearish bet—reflects rational reassessment of overvalued proprietary models, not technophobia.

4. US sanctions backfired, accelerating Chinese innovation rather than suppressing it.

5. The AI bubble has burst for proprietary models, but AI itself continues advancing—now outside US control.

The implications cascade across technology, economics, and geopolitics. We are witnessing not just a market correction but a paradigm shift in how intelligence is produced, distributed, and governed.

§ Recommendations for Further Research

For Researchers:

  • Conduct independent benchmarking of Chinese open-source models under controlled conditions
  • Investigate actual training costs through infrastructure analysis and energy consumption tracking
  • Develop governance frameworks for uncontrolled AI proliferation
  • Study social impacts of cognitive labor commodification

For Policymakers:

  • Reassess export control strategies that may be counterproductive
  • Develop international AI governance mechanisms that account for open-source reality
  • Invest in AI safety research that works for decentralized models
  • Prepare social safety nets for AI-driven labor disruption

For Investors:

  • Reevaluate AI company valuations in light of open-source competition
  • Consider positions that benefit from AI commoditization (inference, integration, services)
  • Monitor Chinese model releases as leading indicators of capability trends
  • Assess exposure to companies dependent on proprietary AI moats

For Citizens:

  • Understand that AI governance is becoming impossible through traditional channels
  • Demand transparency about AI use in institutions that affect your life
  • Learn to use open-source AI tools to reduce dependency on corporate platforms
  • Participate in debates about AI's role in democracy before those debates become moot
⚠ Critical Uncertainties ⚠

This investigation acknowledges several limitations and uncertainties:

  • Training Cost Verification: The $4.6M figure for Kimi K2 cannot be independently confirmed. However, even if actual costs were 10x higher, the economic argument holds.
  • Benchmark Gaming: Chinese labs may optimize specifically for publicized benchmarks. Real-world performance could differ from test scores.
  • Hidden Costs: Open-source models impose inference costs on users. Total cost of ownership may exceed API pricing for some use cases.
  • US Lab Response: OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google may yet achieve breakthroughs that restore their moat. This report reflects November 2025 conditions.
  • Regulatory Wildcards: Government intervention could reshape the landscape unpredictably. Scenario B remains possible.

This report was produced through human-AI collaboration. The human investigator provided source materials, strategic direction, and critical theory framing. The AI model (Claude Sonnet 4.5) contributed structural organization, prose generation, and analytical synthesis.

Known Biases:

  • Emphasis on English-language sources may underweight Chinese-language discussions
  • Critical theory lens reflects Western academic traditions
  • Tech industry focus may neglect perspectives from affected workers and communities
  • Reliance on public benchmarks may miss proprietary performance data

Methodological Limitations:

  • Market analysis relies on public filings; private positions unknown
  • Geopolitical analysis reflects incomplete information about state strategies
  • Testimony is anecdotal and represents individual user experience
  • Future scenarios are speculative by definition

On AI Collaboration:

The use of AI to investigate AI creates inherent reflexivity. This report could not have been produced without the very technologies it analyzes. Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic) is a proprietary model—a product of the US incumbents this report suggests may be overvalued. This creates potential conflicts:

  • Does the AI have "interests" in defending proprietary models?
  • Does its training data reflect Silicon Valley perspectives disproportionately?
  • Can it objectively analyze competitors like Kimi K2 that may threaten its own relevance?

We acknowledge these tensions without claiming to fully resolve them. Transparency about the collaboration is itself a form of evidence: the report exists because AI has become infrastructure, inescapable even in critical investigations of AI.

§ On the Limits of Neutrality

This investigation does not claim neutrality. It takes a position: that open-source disruption challenges incumbent power structures, and that this challenge has geopolitical, economic, and ethical dimensions worth examining critically.

Alternative framings exist:

  • National security hawks view Chinese AI as an existential threat requiring containment
  • AI safety researchers worry open-source accelerates capabilities without adequate safeguards
  • Labor advocates see all AI—proprietary or open—as tools of worker displacement
  • Tech optimists believe competition will drive beneficial innovation regardless of origin

Each perspective contains truth. This report privileges economic and geopolitical analysis through a critical theory lens because those dimensions are underexplored in mainstream coverage. But it does not claim to be the only valid interpretation.
