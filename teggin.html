The Huguenot Exodus • 1689

Pierre de Villiers was a French Huguenot who fled religious persecution, arriving at the Cape of Good Hope on May 6, 1689, aboard the ship Zion with his brothers Abraham and Jacques. Originally from Burgundy and later La Rochelle, they were experienced viticulturists who brought their knowledge of winemaking to South Africa.

The brothers departed from the Dutch port of Texel on January 8, 1689, carrying little more than their clothes and family Bibles. The Chamber of Delft specifically recommended them to Governor Simon van der Stel for their expertise in vine cultivation. Together, they planted over 40,000 vines at the Cape.

Pierre first farmed at La Rochelle with his brothers, then received the farm "Bourgogne" in Franschhoek in 1694, and later settled on "Picardie et La Brie" in Paarl. He served as Church Deacon and Elder in Drakenstein and was among the 240 burghers who signed the famous petition against government corruption led by Adam Tas.

In 1714, Johannes Guillaume de Gravenbroek, Secretary to the Government, bequeathed Pierre his gold ring set with garnets "in acknowledgement of kindness received from his co-religionists in France." Pierre died in 1720 and was buried in an unmarked grave at the Rietdak Church cemetery in Paarl.

The De Villiers Dynasty

Louis Libert Guillaume Marc de Villiers represents one of the deepest documented roots of your family tree, born in the same era as Philibert Faure. The De Villiers family of Saint-Martin-du-Lac, Burgundy, were established French nobility who later became Huguenot Protestants. Their descendants would flee France during the religious persecutions of the 1680s, bringing their heritage and skills to the Cape of Good Hope, where the De Villiers became one of South Africa's most prominent founding families.

About This Archive

This family tree spans nearly 500 years and 15 generations, tracing back to two parallel lineages both originating in the 1530s-1540s: Philibert Faure (ca. 1530) in France and Louis Libert Guillaume Marc de Villiers (1540) in Burgundy. The Teggin line represents English heritage from Georgian Britain (ca. 1787), while both the Faure and De Villiers lines represent French Huguenot ancestry.

The De Villiers story is particularly remarkable: Pierre de Villiers and his brothers fled religious persecution in France, arriving at the Cape of Good Hope in 1689 with little more than their Bibles and their expertise in viticulture. They became founding fathers of South African winemaking, establishing farms in Franschhoek and Paarl that helped shape the Cape's wine industry. The De Villiers remain one of South Africa's most prominent Huguenot families.
