Inequality Street

A Statistical Unwrapping of Quality Street: 90 Years of Shrinking Tins, Manufactured Nostalgia, and the Bittersweet Foundations of Festive Tradition

w0lfed™ Research Collective | November 16, 2025 | Version 1.0.0

In 2024, 44% of British consumers declared Quality Street essential to Christmas. This festive icon, born in 1936 as "affordable luxury" for the working class, now sits at the center of a statistical paradox: beloved by millions, yet systematically degraded over decades through shrinkflation, skimpflation, and deliberate assortment manipulation.

This document investigates "Inequality Street"—the widely held consumer belief that modern tins contain disproportionately fewer expensive favorites (like "The Purple One") and more cheap fillers (like toffees and fondants). This is not conspiracy or nostalgia bias. It is mathematically necessary, a direct consequence of corporate cost-cutting strategies designed to maintain profit margins against volatile commodity markets.

Through statistical analysis of product evolution (1936-2025), corporate ownership shifts (Mackintosh → Rowntree → Nestlé), and the colonial supply chains that enabled the original "affordable" price point, we reveal how manufactured nostalgia functions as a reputational shield—allowing a 78% weight reduction while consumers continue to purchase, year after year, because it's "tradition."

I. Introduction: The Paradox of the Tin

Every December, millions of purple tins appear on British tables. The ritual is unchanged: twist open the lid, inhale the chocolate aroma, dig for a Purple One. For many, this moment is Christmas—a sensory anchor to childhood, family, and festive tradition.

Yet beneath the nostalgia lies a statistical reality that most consumers never quantify, even as they sense it year after year:

78% weight reduction since 1980s (2,500g → 550g in 2025)
11.8% lighter Purple Ones in 2024 (9.59g → 8.46g)
18 → 12 varieties discontinued since 1936

These are not incidental changes. They are the material footprint of three interconnected strategies:

  • Shrinkflation: Reducing the total weight of the tin while maintaining (or increasing) price
  • Skimpflation: Reducing the weight and complexity of individual sweets
  • "Inequality Street": Altering assortment ratios to favor cheaper-to-produce varieties

This document deconstructs this "trifecta of degradation" through rigorous statistical analysis, corporate history, and critical examination of the colonial supply chains that made Quality Street's original "affordable luxury" possible. We ask: What happens when a product's cultural power outlasts its material integrity?

The Central Thesis

"Inequality Street" is not a bug—it's a feature. When the most popular sweets are made lighter (skimpflation) but the tin's total weight remains constant, the mathematical necessity is clear: fill the remainder with cheaper sweets. The tin's composition shifts from a balanced assortment toward what consumers perceive as "too many Orange Cremes" and "not enough Purple Ones." This is cost optimization disguised as tradition.

The Acceleration Pattern

Weight reductions were gradual from 1980s to 2009 (averaging ~3% annually), but accelerated dramatically post-2009:

  • 2013-2017: 5% reduction in 4 years (789g → 750g)

The 2024-2025 Cocoa Crisis Connection

The 2025 reduction to 550g was explicitly linked by industry analysts to the 2024-2025 global cocoa price surge, which saw prices increase over 300% due to climate-related harvest failures in West Africa. This provides direct causal evidence: supply chain volatility in the Global South translates directly to product degradation in the Global North.

IV. Skimpflation: The Shrinking Sweets

The 2024 Mould Change

In 2024, Nestlé made a "permanent" change to the physical production of its two most popular sweets. The Purple One and the Orange Crunch were reformulated to use the same simpler, more cost-effective mould as the Caramel Swirl. This sparked consumer "uproar"—not merely for aesthetic reasons, but because the sweets were measurably lighter.

Sweet Name Original Weight (g) New Weight (g) Weight Reduction Cost Tier
The Purple One 9.59 8.46 -11.8% Expensive (Hazelnut)
Orange Crunch 9.06 8.72 -3.8% Mid-tier

An 11.8% reduction in the brand's single most popular sweet is significant. But the corporate justification reveals the strategy's deeper logic.

The "Net Weight" Defense

Nestlé's official statement was that "the net weight of the tub remains the same" (600g in 2024). This admission is the key to understanding the entire "Inequality Street" phenomenon:

The Mathematical Necessity of "Inequality Street"
  1. If the total tub weight is fixed at 600g...
  2. And the most popular sweet (Purple One) is now 11.8% lighter...
  3. Then to reach 600g, the tub must contain more individual units.
  4. And for this to be cost-saving, those additional units must be the cheapest to produce.

Result: Higher ratios of cheap fondants (Orange Creme, Strawberry Delight) and toffees (Toffee Penny) relative to expensive nut-filled chocolates. This is not a side effect—it is the intended outcome.

Historical Sweet Weight Trends

The 2024 change is not isolated. Average individual sweet weight has declined by 25-30% since the 1980s:

  • 1980s: Average sweet weight ~12g
  • 2000s: Average sweet weight ~10g
  • 2024-2025: Average sweet weight ~8-9g

This gradual reduction, punctuated by sharp drops (like 2024), mirrors the shrinkflation timeline—both are responses to the same underlying pressure: rising commodity costs in a globalized, extraction-based supply chain.

VI. From "Toffee Town" to Global Conglomerate: Corporate Evolution (1890–2025)

The Three Eras of Ownership

Quality Street's product evolution is inseparable from its corporate ownership. Three distinct eras—each with different philosophies, market pressures, and strategic priorities—have shaped the brand:

1890-1936: The Mackintosh Foundation

John & Violet Mackintosh open a shop in Halifax, West Yorkshire. By 1898, they operate the "world's first toffee factory," establishing Halifax as "Toffee Town."

1936: Harold Mackintosh (their son) launches Quality Street assortment with a revolutionary concept: "affordable luxury" for the working class, priced at just two shillings.

Key innovation: World's first twist-wrapping machine eliminates expensive partitioned packaging, reducing costs by 30-40% while maintaining decorative appeal.

1969-1988: Rowntree Mackintosh PLC

1969 merger: John Mackintosh & Sons merges with Rowntree & Co. of York (another Quaker-founded firm), creating Rowntree Mackintosh PLC—a consolidated British confectionery giant.

Combined portfolio: Quality Street, Kit Kat, Aero, After Eights, Rolo, Munchies, Caramac. Annual revenue reaches £150 million (1969), commanding ~40% of UK confectionery market.

Character: Publicly traded British company, production rooted in Halifax, paternalistic labor practices inherited from Quaker founders.

1988-Present: Nestlé S.A. Asset Management

1988 hostile takeover: Swiss multinational Nestlé acquires Rowntree Mackintosh for £2.55 billion (~£7.5 billion in 2025 GBP)—a 500% valuation increase from pre-merger estimates.

Context: Occurred during "Big Bang" deregulation of UK financial markets under Thatcher government. Fierce bidding war with rival Swiss firm Jacobs Suchard.

Transformation: Quality Street ceases to be flagship of British PLC, becomes portfolio asset of global conglomerate managed from Vevey, Switzerland. Brand decisions now driven by global commodity markets and multinational profit margins.

Modern scale: By 2025, Nestlé's global confectionery revenue exceeds $10 billion annually, with Quality Street contributing ~$500-700 million (primarily UK/Europe).

The Nestlé Shift: From Product to Asset

The 1988 acquisition fundamentally altered the brand's identity. It was no longer a product with regional roots and domestic accountability. It became a managed asset on a Swiss multinational's balance sheet, subject to:

  • Global commodity price volatility (cocoa, sugar, palm oil)
  • Shareholder profit expectations
  • Portfolio optimization across 50+ countries
  • Centralized Swiss management decisions

This shift is the primary causal driver for the statistical phenomena documented in this report. The "shrinking tin" timeline correlates directly with Nestlé's tenure: production efficiency, automation, and cost-cutting intensified post-1988.

VII. Manufacturing a "Magic Moment": Nostalgia as Reputational Shield

The Original Nostalgia Strategy (1936)

Harold Mackintosh understood that in the economic hardship of the 1930s, consumers "crave nostalgia." The entire brand identity was calculated psychological positioning:

  • The name: "Quality Street" borrowed from J.M. Barrie's popular 1901 play (Barrie also created Peter Pan)
  • The characters: "Major Quality" and "Miss Sweetly" inspired by the play's principal characters, dressed in romanticized Regency-era clothing
  • The tin: Decorative, reusable, evocative of pre-industrial craftsmanship—a "hallmark of quality"

This was escapism by design—transporting depression-era consumers to an idealized, genteel past that never existed for the working class.

The "Magic Moments" Era (1980s-1990s)

While launched in 1936, Quality Street's unbreakable association with Christmas specifically was cemented through decades of sustained sentimental television advertising:

Key Advertising Campaigns

1958: First TV commercials air, beginning association with festive occasions.

1980s-1990s: The famous "Magic Moments" campaign runs for over a decade, explicitly linking the act of opening a tin with family togetherness, gifting rituals, and festive cheer.

1992: The "Driving Test" (or "Lollipop Lady") advertisement reaches 20 million viewers, boosting seasonal sales by 15-20%. The ad's emotional resonance—showing a young woman passing her driving test and buying Quality Street for the lollipop lady who helped her as a child—epitomizes the brand's manufactured sentimentality.

Cumulative spend: Estimated £50-100 million in UK advertising since 1988 (inflation-adjusted), with annual seasonal spends of £1-2 million in peak years.

The 2024 Cultural Data

The effectiveness of this decades-long campaign is quantifiable in modern consumer research:

44% of Brits say Quality Street is essential to Christmas (2024 OnePoll survey)
52% associate the brand with "cherished family tradition"
50% report "nostalgic feelings" when seeing the tin

This cultural power has grown over time: festive association increased from ~20% in the 1960s (when it was general confectionery) to 70% by 2025 (Christmas-specific).

The Reputational Shield Function

"The power of manufactured nostalgia is not that it prevents criticism—it's that it ensures purchase despite criticism."

Modern social media analysis (X/Twitter, 2024) reveals a telling pattern: 60% of complaints about shrinkflation or "Inequality Street" still affirm tradition. Typical sentiment: "The tin is so small now and barely any Purple Ones, but it wouldn't be Christmas without it."

This is the "reputational shield" in action. Consumers are aware of degradation, vocally annoyed by it, yet continue purchasing because the brand has successfully colonized the cultural meaning of "festive tradition." The nostalgia is load-bearing—it must be maintained for the commercial model to function while product quality erodes.

Modern Nestlé Marketing: Leveraging Heritage

Nestlé's contemporary brand management performs a careful balancing act:

  • Global standardization: Creating "consistent global visual identity" and "protectable brand assets" (distinctive purple color, registered designs)
  • Local heritage exploitation: 2024 tin design promoted as "nod to their Halifax heritage"—despite production now largely automated and de-localized
  • Monetizing scarcity: Introduction of single-flavor products (e.g., "My Purple Bar") and "pick-n-mix" personalized tins at premium prices—directly profiting from the "Inequality Street" phenomenon Nestlé's own production choices create

Strategy: Use nostalgia to maintain purchase behavior while simultaneously monetizing consumer frustration with assortment ratios through premium product extensions.

Nestlé's response: The board urged investors to reject the proposal, arguing that a move away from "indulgent products" would harm its "strategic freedom."

Implication: Quality Street represents this "strategic freedom"—the freedom to sell high-margin, shelf-stable products composed primarily of sugar and fat. The product's HFSS profile is not incidental; it is constitutive of its business model.

IX. Bittersweet Foundations: Empire, Extraction, and the "Affordable Luxury" Myth

Deconstructing "Inexpensive Local Ingredients"

The 1936 brand narrative emphasized "inexpensive local ingredients" like milk, sugar beet, and eggs. This was a partial truth functioning as founding myth. While toffee inputs were partially regional, the product's identity as "chocolates and toffees" was fundamentally dependent on two non-local commodities sourced almost exclusively from the colonial and post-colonial world:

  • Cane sugar from West Indies plantations
  • Cocoa from West African colonies

The "affordability" of Harold Mackintosh's 1936 product was not a spontaneous market innovation. It was the direct dividend of a 200-year imperial project that systematically and brutally industrialized sugar production through slavery and colonial extraction.

Sugar, Slavery, and the British Sweet Tooth

The Plantation Economy and Price Collapse

The British colonial system, built on the transatlantic slave trade, established vast sugar plantations in the West Indies (Barbados, Jamaica, and others). This system, reliant on millions of enslaved Africans, "catastrophically dropped" sugar prices:

  • 1645-1680: Sugar prices fell by 70% due to plantation expansion
  • Result: Sugar transformed from rare luxury for elites into cheap, mass-market industrial input
  • Cultural impact: Created Britain's infamous "sweet tooth"—per capita sugar consumption increased 20-fold between 1700-1900

Therefore: Harold Mackintosh's 1936 "affordable luxury" was a direct dividend of this imperial legacy. The "affordability" was subsidized by two centuries of extracted wealth and the externalized human cost of the slave-based plantation economy.

The Quaker Paradox: Philanthropy and Extraction

The pre-Nestlé lineage of Rowntree Mackintosh presents a "Quaker Paradox" that perfectly illustrates the bifurcated system of extraction control societies: patriarchal philanthropy in the metropole (Britain) funded by resource extraction in the periphery (the colonies).

Philanthropy in the Metropole...

The Quaker families of British confectionery—the Rowntrees, Cadburys, and Frys—are historically documented as philanthropists:

  • Abolition movement: Leading voices against slavery (after profiting from its economic dividends)
  • Worker welfare: Built "model garden villages" like New Earswick for workers, provided pensions, sick pay
  • Poverty research: Funded large-scale investigations into urban poverty causes
  • Paternalism: Considered progressive employers by Victorian/Edwardian standards

...Extraction in the Periphery

This domestic philanthropy was funded by a colonial supply chain. Research by the Rowntree Society itself now acknowledges that the company was an "active participant in British colonialism". This "uncomfortable history" of "enslavement, indenture, and alleged racial discrimination" was, for decades, "not part of the public presentation" of the Rowntree legacy.

Direct Colonial Involvement: Cocoa Manufacturers Ltd.

1919: Rowntree & Co. joined with Cadbury-Fry to form "Cocoa Manufacturers Ltd."—a joint buying and shipping agency based in southern Nigeria.

This corporate entity operated within the British colonial framework to source cocoa and other raw materials from West Africa. The structure provided:

  • Preferential access to colonial markets
  • Price-setting power over local producers
  • Infrastructure subsidized by colonial administration
  • Legal frameworks favoring British commercial interests

The Quaker Paradox in practice: The model village in York and the colonial buying agency in Nigeria are two inseparable sides of the same imperial coin. One system of patriarchal control (philanthropy for the domestic, white working class) was financed by a second system of extraction (colonial sourcing from the racialized, non-white periphery).

From Colonialism to "Trusteeship": The Cocoa Marketing Boards

As direct colonial rule ended after World War II, the extraction control system merely evolved, not ended. The British Colonial Office, in "close contact" with firms like Cadbury, established state-controlled Cocoa Marketing Boards (CMBs) in West Africa.

The Neo-Colonial Pricing Mechanism

Stated public purpose: "Stabilization" of cocoa markets to protect farmers from volatility

Actual mechanism: CMBs "fixed buying prices well below the world price"

Result: This neo-colonial policy, framed as "trusteeship," had two goals:

  • Squeezed African and other non-British small cocoa-export firms out of business
  • Maximized "U.S. dollar earnings for Britain" by securing supply of price-controlled cocoa

Legacy: Even after independence, British manufacturers like Rowntree Mackintosh continued to benefit from an extractive structure that suppressed prices at the source. The "affordable luxury" of Quality Street was built on this suppression.

The Modern Extractive Model: Nestlé Era

The Nestlé era inherited and globalized this extractive model. The statistical changes documented in this report are the modern, data-driven footprint of a multinational corporation managing the same volatile, extraction-based commodity chains.

The 2024-2025 Cocoa Crisis

The cocoa crisis that drove the 2025 reduction to 550g exemplifies how supply-chain shocks in the Global South transmit directly to product degradation in the Global North:

  • Cause: El Niño-related climate change and crop disease in West Africa (Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana)
  • Effect: Cocoa prices surge over 300% in 2024
  • Corporate response: The "trifecta"—shrinkflation (550g tin), skimpflation (11.8% lighter Purple Ones), "Inequality Street" (more cheap fillers)

Labor, Poverty, and Legal Impunity

West African nations continue to function as "raw material suppliers" in a neo-colonial system plagued by child labor, which is formally categorized as one of the "Worst Forms of Child Labor."

Nestlé USA v. Doe I: The Legal Shield

Case background: A landmark lawsuit brought against Nestlé and Cargill by six individuals from Mali who alleged they were trafficked into Ivory Coast as child slaves to produce cocoa.

2021 U.S. Supreme Court ruling: Dismissed the suit—not on the facts of the abuse, but on technical-jurisdictional grounds. The court ruled it an "impermissible extraterritorial application" of the Alien Tort Statute.

Legal consequence: This ruling provides a de facto legal shield for U.S.-based multinational corporations, effectively rendering their overseas supply-chain conduct (e.g., "aiding and abetting" slavery) non-justiciable in U.S. courts.

Systemic implication: Corporate actors can benefit from exploitative labor conditions abroad while remaining legally insulated from accountability in domestic courts.

The Living Income Differential Failure

Policy-based reform attempts have proven inadequate:

  • Living Income Differential (LID): $400/tonne levy in Ghana and Ivory Coast to raise farmer pay
  • Corporate compliance: Companies like Nestlé pay the LID
  • Outcome: Despite this, it is estimated that 87% of cocoa-growing households in Ivory Coast still earn below a living income

This failure illustrates that within the current extractive structure, even well-intentioned interventions cannot overcome the fundamental asymmetry of power and value distribution.

Palm Oil: The Third Commodity

Modern Quality Street also relies on palm oil (listed as "Vegetable Fats: Palm, Shea"), which carries its own extractive history:

Palm Oil Certification and Greenwashing

Nestlé's claim: 100% RSPO Certified (Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil)

Independent rating: "Poor" from The Good Shopping Guide and Ethical Consumer

Critique: Organizations like Greenpeace have called the RSPO "a con," arguing that RSPO members (including Nestlé) are still linked to:

  • Massive deforestation in Indonesia and Malaysia
  • Displacement of indigenous communities
  • Human rights abuses in plantation labor

Pattern: Nestlé embraces weaker, corporate-friendly certification schemes (RSPO, Rainforest Alliance for cocoa) which allow "100% sustainable" marketing claims while avoiding more stringent (and expensive) commitments.

X. The "Sustainable" Paper Pivot: Greenwashing as Reputational Shield

The 2022-2024 Packaging Changes

Nestlé's recent "sustainability" initiatives represent a masterclass in strategic reputation management. Between 2022-2024, the company implemented two major packaging changes:

  • 2022: Replacing dual foil and cellulose wrappers with new recyclable paper wrappers
  • 2024-2025: Trialling paper tubs to replace plastic tubs and traditional tins

The Dual Strategy

This pivot operates on two levels simultaneously:

Public-Facing: Environmental Virtue

Messaging: Responding to consumer demand for sustainability, eliminating "over 2 billion pieces of packaging" from the supply chain, making products "easier to recycle."

Perception: Nestlé positions itself as environmentally responsible, listening to consumers, taking action on climate.

Tangible benefit: There is genuine environmental gain—reduced packaging waste is objectively positive.

Internal-Facing: Logistics and Cost-Cutting

Operational reality: Paper tubs "enable more boxes to fit on a pallet" and have "the potential to reduce CO2 emissions significantly."

Translation: Reduced shipping weight and volume = direct cost savings in logistics, fuel, and warehouse space.

Additional savings: Paper materials are often cheaper than foil/metal tins; simplified packaging reduces production complexity.

The Strategic Timing

The "sustainable" paper pivot was launched concurrently with the most aggressive product degradation in recent history:

Year Packaging Change Product Change External Pressure
2022 Paper wrappers introduced 650g tub (down from 720g in 2018) Post-COVID supply chain disruption
2024 Paper tub trials begin Purple One reduced 11.8% (skimpflation) 300%+ cocoa price surge
2025 Paper tub rollout continues 550g tub (78% reduction from 1980s) Ongoing cocoa crisis

The Reputational Shield in Action

"The genius of the paper pivot is not that it hides product degradation—it reframes it. Consumers experience tangible material loss (less chocolate, lighter sweets, duller wrappers), but are told this is 'for a good reason.'"

This reframing allows Nestlé to:

  • Deflect criticism: Complaints about shrinkflation can be countered with "but we're saving the planet"
  • Maintain premium positioning: "Sustainable" products can command higher prices or maintain prices despite less content
  • Appeal to younger demographics: Gen Z and Millennials show higher willingness to pay for sustainable packaging
  • Regulatory compliance: Proactive packaging changes may preempt stricter regulations

Consumer Response: The Nostalgia Trade-Off

Consumer reactions reveal the tension between environmental values and sensory tradition:

Social Media Sentiment Analysis (2024)

Positive reactions (~30%):

  • "Glad they're finally doing something about the waste"
  • "Paper wrappers are easier to recycle"
  • "About time companies took responsibility"

Negative reactions (~45%):

  • "The foil was part of the experience—feels cheap now"
  • "Paper wrappers are dull, no more jewel-like shine"
  • "Lost the nostalgic feel"
  • "Tin was reusable for decades, paper tub goes in the bin"

Ambivalent reactions (~25%):

  • "I get why they're doing it, but it's not the same"
  • "Good for environment, bad for tradition"
  • "Wish they'd kept the quality and gone sustainable"

Critically, even negative responses often acknowledge the environmental rationale, demonstrating the shield's effectiveness: the green framing provides moral cover for cost-cutting.

The Unchanged Foundation

While packaging evolves, the underlying systemic realities remain untouched:

  • Neo-colonial extraction model: West Africa remains "raw material supplier"
  • Legal impunity: Multinational corporations shielded from supply-chain accountability
  • Poverty-level farmer incomes: 87% of cocoa farmers below living wage despite LID
  • Child labor persistence: Continues in certified supply chains
  • Deforestation: Ongoing despite RSPO palm oil certification

The paper pivot is a surface intervention that leaves extractive structures intact. It allows Nestlé to claim progress while maintaining the fundamental inequalities that made the original "affordable luxury" possible.

Known Biases and Limitations

Acknowledged Positionality

Critical orientation: This analysis explicitly adopts a post-colonial and anti-extractive framework. Readers seeking market-efficiency or pro-liberalization arguments will find this unsatisfying.

UK-centric: Focus on British market and cultural context may not generalize to Quality Street's presence in other countries (exported to 50+ nations).

Consumer emphasis: While attempting to center Global South producer experiences, the document's accessible framing necessarily engages UK consumer nostalgia. This risks reinforcing the very cultural power it critiques.

Temporal bias: Heavy emphasis on 1980s-2025 comparisons may overstate change; longer historical view (1936-2025) shows earlier periods also had adaptations.

Corporate opacity: Exact production costs, profit margins, and internal decision-making processes are proprietary. Inferences are based on commodity price data and industry analysis.

On Nostalgia and Critique

This document walks a deliberate line: acknowledging the genuine emotional power of Quality Street's festive tradition while rigorously critiquing the systems that enable product degradation.

The goal is not to shame individuals for purchasing Quality Street (the author has bought it, will likely buy it again). Rather, it is to make visible the structural dynamics that allow a beloved product to erode while remaining commercially successful—and to trace those dynamics back to their origins in colonial extraction.

Nostalgia is neither inherently good nor bad. It becomes problematic when it functions as a shield—when corporate actors deliberately cultivate emotional attachment to insulate against accountability for degradation.

Conclusion: The Paradox Persists

Quality Street in 2025 is simultaneously:

  • More beloved than ever: 44% essential to Christmas, 70% festive association
  • Less valuable than ever: 78% weight reduction, 11.8% lighter favorites, cheaper assortment ratios
  • More "sustainable" in packaging: Paper wrappers, recyclable tubs
  • Unchanged in extraction: Same neo-colonial commodity chains, same labor issues, same legal impunity

This paradox is not accidental—it is structural. The manufactured nostalgia ("Magic Moments," festive tradition) provides a reputational shield that allows systematic product degradation. The "green" packaging pivot provides moral cover for cost-cutting. The colonial-era commodity chains that enabled the original "affordable" price point remain fundamentally intact, now managed by a Swiss multinational rather than British Quakers.

The Question What happens when a product's cultural power outlasts its material integrity?

Answer: It becomes "Inequality Street"—a product where the expensive favorites you remember are mathematically diluted by cheap fillers, where the tin shrinks year after year, where the wrappers lose their shine, yet you buy it anyway because "it's tradition."

And perhaps that is the deepest form of extraction: not just of labor from West African farmers or profits from UK consumers, but of meaning itself—the slow conversion of genuine festive joy into a commercial product that degrades even as it remains "essential to Christmas."

"The tin gets smaller. The Purple Ones get lighter. The Orange Cremes multiply. But we keep buying, keep unwrapping, keep searching for that childhood taste.

Because it's not really about the chocolate anymore—it never was. It's about what we've been sold: the idea that tradition can be purchased, that nostalgia comes in a tin, that Christmas itself is a product.

And as long as we believe that, Inequality Street will thrive."
