Executive Summary

What Is This Document?

This is a living, open-source reference guide for anyone working — or beginning to work — a 12-step program through Alcoholics Anonymous (AA), Narcotics Anonymous (NA), or any related fellowship. It is designed to grow, evolve, and be forked.

The 12 Steps were first written in 1939 and published in the book Alcoholics Anonymous (the "Big Book"). They were later adapted by Narcotics Anonymous, founded in 1953, with modified language to address all forms of addiction rather than alcohol specifically. Both programs have helped tens of millions of people around the world find freedom from addiction.

"Rarely have we seen a person fail who has thoroughly followed our path." — Alcoholics Anonymous, "How It Works"

This document covers: the complete 12 Steps for both AA and NA, a comparison of how the language differs between programs, in-depth step work questions for each step, spiritual principles, key prayers and meditations, program slogans, meeting formats, sponsorship guidance, and a comprehensive resource directory.

How to Use This Guide

Work this guide alongside a sponsor, not instead of one. A sponsor is an experienced member of the fellowship who guides you through the steps. This document is a reference and a companion — it does not replace the lived community of recovery.

Step Work

In-Depth Step Work Guide

Each step below includes the official text for both AA and NA, a description of its purpose, and reflection questions drawn from widely used recovery workbooks and literature. Work these questions in writing, with your sponsor.

01

Admission & Surrender

AA We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable.
NA We admitted that we were powerless over our addiction, that our lives had become unmanageable.

Step One is about breaking through denial. Addiction creates a powerful illusion of control. This step asks us to look honestly at the facts — our lives and our relationship with substances have become unmanageable, no matter how hard we tried to manage them. The "WE" at the beginning of every step is intentional: this is a program of community, not isolation.

Step 1 Work Questions

  1. How did you first discover your addiction? What was your relationship with it in the beginning?
  2. Describe the progression of your use. How did it change over time?
  3. List specific ways your life became unmanageable as a result of your addiction.
  4. Have you tried to control or stop your use before? What happened?
  5. What does "powerless" mean to you? Do you accept it applies to you?
  6. How has your addiction affected your relationships, work, finances, health, and spirituality?
  7. What did you lose or nearly lose because of your addiction?
  8. What were the consequences — legal, personal, physical — of your use?
  9. What kept you coming back, even when you knew it was hurting you?
  10. Write out the benefits of accepting your powerlessness over addiction. List at least five.
02

Coming to Hope

AA Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity.
NA We came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity.

Step Two is not a religious step — it is a spiritual one. It asks only for openness to the idea that something larger than your own willpower exists. Your Higher Power can be the collective wisdom of the group, nature, love, or any conception that gives you hope and direction. The "insanity" referred to is the insanity of expecting different results from the same behaviors.

Step 2 Work Questions

  1. What is your current concept of a Higher Power? Has it changed since you came to recovery?
  2. What does "sanity" mean to you? Describe what a sane life might look like.
  3. How has your addiction caused you to act in ways you now consider insane or self-destructive?
  4. Have you ever had any spiritual experiences? What were they?
  5. What is your earliest memory of a belief — or disbelief — in a Higher Power?
  6. Are you willing to believe that something greater than yourself could help you? What would that take?
  7. What fears or resentments do you have about religion or spirituality?
  8. How have you seen the program work for others? Can you believe it might work for you?
03

Decision & Surrender

AA Made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him.
NA We made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him.

Steps 1–3 are often described as the "foundation" steps. If Step 1 is about seeing the problem, and Step 2 is about opening to a solution, Step 3 is about acting on that opening. This step is a decision, not a feeling — it is made in the present and renewed daily. Many members say the Third Step Prayer each morning.

Step 3 Work Questions

  1. What does "turning your will over" mean to you in practical terms?
  2. In what areas of your life do you most resist letting go of control?
  3. What is the difference between self-will and God's will (or your Higher Power's will)?
  4. How has self-will run riot shown up in your life?
  5. What would it look like to fully trust your Higher Power with your future?
  6. Write out the Third Step Prayer in your own words. What does it mean to you?
  7. Are you willing to make this decision today — even if you don't fully understand it yet?
04

The Moral Inventory

AA Made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.
NA We made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.

Step Four is often the step people fear most — and the one that changes the most lives. This is a written inventory. The Big Book suggests writing resentments, fears, harms to others, and sexual conduct. The NA Step Working Guide expands this into a full exploration of patterns, defects, and assets. This is not a punishment — it is a preparation for freedom.

The 4-Column Method (AA Big Book): Column 1 — Who or what I resent. Column 2 — The cause. Column 3 — What it affects (self-esteem, finances, relations, etc.). Column 4 — My part in it.

Step 4 Work Questions

  1. Write a complete list of everyone and everything you resent. Be thorough.
  2. For each resentment: what happened, and what part of you did it threaten?
  3. What was your part? How did you contribute, even if only a little?
  4. List your fears. What do you fear losing? What do you fear never having?
  5. How have you harmed others — emotionally, financially, physically, sexually?
  6. What character defects do you notice repeating across your resentments and harms?
  7. What are your assets? What do you value in yourself? What do others value?
  8. Are there any secrets you have never told anyone? Write them here.
05

Sharing the Inventory

AA Admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs.
NA We admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs.

The Fifth Step is the breaking of isolation. We read our Fourth Step inventory aloud to our sponsor — or a trusted person. This step is not confession for punishment; it is the act of being completely known and completely accepted. Most people report a profound feeling of relief upon completing it. The Big Book says that when we withhold nothing, "we can look the world in the eye."

Step 5 Work Questions

  1. Who will you share your Fifth Step with? Have you chosen your sponsor for this?
  2. What are you most afraid to disclose?
  3. What would it mean to you to be fully known and still accepted?
  4. After completing Step 5, how do you feel? What shifted?
  5. Did anything surprise you about the experience of being heard?
  6. Are there any wrongs you held back? Why?

Steps Six Through Twelve

Step 6 — Readiness: "Were entirely ready to have God remove all these defects of character."

Step Six is a step of willingness. After seeing our patterns in Step 4 and speaking them in Step 5, we now become willing to let them go. This doesn't mean they disappear immediately — it means we stop clinging to them. Ask yourself: am I really ready to give up my resentment, my pride, my fear-based behaviors — even if they have served as armor?

Step 6 Work Questions

  1. List your character defects from Step 4. Are you truly willing for all of them to be removed?
  2. Which defects are you most reluctant to release? Why?
  3. How do these defects still "serve" you — what do they protect you from?
  4. What would life look like without your most persistent defects?
  5. What does "entirely ready" mean — does it require perfection or just willingness?
Step 7 — Humility: "Humbly asked Him to remove our shortcomings."

The Seventh Step is a prayer of humility. After readiness in Step 6, we ask our Higher Power to remove the defects of character that are blocking our growth. This step requires the humility to admit we cannot change ourselves by willpower alone, and the trust that something greater is working in our lives. The Seventh Step Prayer is one of the most important prayers in the program.

Step 7 Work Questions

  1. What does humility mean to you? How is it different from humiliation?
  2. Have you prayed or asked your Higher Power to remove your shortcomings? What was that like?
  3. What shortcomings have you noticed being gradually lifted since working this step?
  4. Which shortcomings continue to surface? Are you still willing for them to be removed?
  5. How does practicing humility show up differently in your day-to-day life?
Step 8 — The List: "Made a list of all persons we had harmed, and became willing to make amends to them all."

Step Eight asks us to compile a complete list of people we have harmed and — crucially — become willing to make amends. The willingness matters as much as the list. This step is about changing our relationship to the harm we have caused, not about rushing to act. The amends come in Step 9. For now: write the list, sit with it, and cultivate willingness.

Step 8 Work Questions

  1. Write a complete list of all people you have harmed — emotionally, financially, physically, sexually, through neglect.
  2. For each person, what was the harm? Be specific.
  3. Are there people on the list you are not yet willing to make amends to? What is the resistance?
  4. Should you be on your own list? How have you harmed yourself?
  5. What does "willing" mean here — do you need to feel it fully before proceeding?
Step 9 — Amends: "Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others."

Step Nine is often described as the most transformative — and the one that produces the famous "9th Step Promises." Amends are not simply apologies; they are a change in behavior. A direct amend means going to the person, acknowledging the harm, and wherever possible, making it right. The exception clause — "except when to do so would injure them or others" — requires consultation with your sponsor before approaching sensitive situations.

Step 9 Work Questions

  1. Go through your Step 8 list. Which amends can you make directly, right now?
  2. Which amends require more time or planning? Which might injure others if made directly?
  3. How will you make the amend — in person, in writing, through action?
  4. What is a "living amend"? How do you plan to change your behavior going forward?
  5. After making amends, what do you notice shifting in your relationships and in yourself?

The 9th Step Promises (AA Big Book, pp. 83–84)

  1. We are going to know a new freedom and a new happiness.
  2. We will not regret the past nor wish to shut the door on it.
  3. We will comprehend the word serenity and we will know peace.
  4. No matter how far down the scale we have gone, we will see how our experience can benefit others.
  5. That feeling of uselessness and self-pity will disappear.
  6. We will lose interest in selfish things and gain interest in our fellows.
  7. Self-seeking will slip away.
  8. Our whole attitude and outlook upon life will change.
  9. Fear of people and of economic insecurity will leave us.
  10. We will intuitively know how to handle situations which used to baffle us.
  11. We will suddenly realize that God is doing for us what we could not do for ourselves.
Step 10 — Daily Inventory: "Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it."

Steps 10–12 are the "maintenance steps" — they are worked for the rest of our lives. Step Ten asks us to continue the inventory process daily. Each evening, we review the day: Was I resentful, selfish, dishonest, or afraid? Do I owe an apology? Was I kind and loving? This daily check keeps our side of the street clean and prevents emotional debt from building up again.

Step 10 Daily Review Questions

  1. Was I resentful today? Toward whom, and why?
  2. Was I selfish, dishonest, or self-seeking in any of my actions?
  3. Was I afraid of anything today? How did I react to that fear?
  4. Do I owe anyone an apology or amend based on today's actions?
  5. Was I of service today? Did I think of others?
  6. What could I have done better? What am I grateful for?
Step 11 — Prayer & Meditation: "Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him…"

Step Eleven is the spiritual maintenance step. Prayer is described as talking to your Higher Power; meditation is listening. This step asks us to deepen our spiritual practice — not to seek specific outcomes, but to align our will with something greater. The closing phrase — "praying only for knowledge of His will for us and the power to carry that out" — is a discipline against self-seeking prayer.

Step 11 Work Questions

  1. What does your current prayer and meditation practice look like?
  2. What is the difference between asking for what you want and asking for guidance?
  3. How has your sense of conscious contact with a Higher Power grown in recovery?
  4. What practices help you most — silence, journaling, movement, reading, nature?
  5. When you pray only for knowledge of God's will — what does that feel like?
  6. How do you recognize guidance in your life?
Step 12 — Service: "Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these Steps, we tried to carry this message…"

Step Twelve is both a culmination and a beginning. The "spiritual awakening" does not require a dramatic conversion experience — for most people it is a gradual shift: a new way of relating to the world, to themselves, to others. The essence of this step is service. Carrying the message means showing up at meetings, sponsoring others, being available, and practicing these principles in every area of life — not just in recovery settings.

Step 12 Work Questions

  1. What does your spiritual awakening look like? How are you different now?
  2. Are you willing to sponsor or work with others in recovery?
  3. How do you carry the message — in meetings, in your home, in your community?
  4. What spiritual principles from the steps do you actively practice today?
  5. In what areas of your life — work, family, friendships — are you still learning to practice these principles?
  6. What does a life in service feel like compared to a life centered on self?
Prayers & Meditations

Core Prayers of the Program

These prayers are the heart of daily practice in both AA and NA. They are not requirements — they are tools. Work with your sponsor and the fellowship to find the practices that connect you to your Higher Power.

The Serenity Prayer

God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, courage to change the things I can, and wisdom to know the difference.

The Third Step Prayer (AA Big Book, p. 63)

God, I offer myself to Thee — to build with me and to do with me as Thou wilt. Relieve me of the bondage of self, that I may better do Thy will. Take away my difficulties, that victory over them may bear witness to those I would help of Thy Power, Thy Love, and Thy Way of life. May I do Thy will always.

The Seventh Step Prayer (AA Big Book, p. 76)

My Creator, I am now willing that you should have all of me, good and bad. I pray that you now remove from me every single defect of character which stands in the way of my usefulness to you and my fellows. Grant me strength, as I go out from here, to do your bidding. Amen.

The St. Francis Prayer (11th Step / Common in AA)

Lord, make me a channel of thy peace — that where there is hatred, I may bring love — that where there is wrong, I may bring the spirit of forgiveness — that where there is discord, I may bring harmony — that where there is error, I may bring truth — that where there is doubt, I may bring faith — that where there is despair, I may bring hope — that where there are shadows, I may bring light — that where there is sadness, I may bring joy. Lord, grant that I may seek rather to comfort, than to be comforted — to understand, than to be understood — to love, than to be loved. For it is by self-forgetting that one finds. It is by forgiving that one is forgiven. It is by dying that one awakens to Eternal Life.

Morning Reflection (Step 11 Practice)

On awakening, let us think about the 24 hours ahead. Consider plans for the day. Before we begin, ask: What can I do today for the person still suffering? Am I asking for self-knowledge and the power to carry it out? Let go of outcomes. Trust the process. Begin.

The 12 Traditions (Fellowship Unity)

The 12 Traditions are to the fellowship what the 12 Steps are to the individual. They protect the unity, autonomy, and integrity of AA and NA groups worldwide. Below are the short form Traditions as used by both AA and NA.

View All 12 Traditions
  1. Our common welfare should come first; personal recovery depends upon AA / NA unity.
  2. For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority — a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience. Our leaders are but trusted servants; they do not govern.
  3. The only requirement for membership is a desire to stop using / drinking.
  4. Each group should be autonomous except in matters affecting other groups or AA / NA as a whole.
  5. Each group has but one primary purpose — to carry its message to the addict / alcoholic who still suffers.
  6. An AA / NA group ought never endorse, finance, or lend the AA / NA name to any related facility or outside enterprise.
  7. Every AA / NA group ought to be fully self-supporting, declining outside contributions.
  8. AA / NA should remain forever non-professional, but our service centers may employ special workers.
  9. AA / NA, as such, ought never be organized; but we may create service boards or committees directly responsible to those they serve.
  10. AA / NA has no opinion on outside issues; hence the AA / NA name ought never be drawn into public controversy.
  11. Our public relations policy is based on attraction rather than promotion; we need always maintain personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films.
  12. Anonymity is the spiritual foundation of all our Traditions, ever reminding us to place principles before personalities.

Reflexive Statement

On This Document

On Design Philosophy

This document was built to democratize access to recovery information — to make the roadmap of 12-step work freely available, readable, and navigable for anyone who needs it. Recovery resources should not be locked behind paywalls, fragmented across websites, or buried in language that feels inaccessible to newcomers.

On AI Collaboration

This document was co-created through human direction and AI assistance. The human author provided conceptual structure, values framing, and recovery-context knowledge. The AI synthesized publicly available recovery literature, step work questions drawn from widely used non-copyrighted resources, and the organizational structure of the Forever Format™ framework.

Known AI Biases: Western-centric and English-language default framing; tendency to over-structure spiritual experience; preference for comprehensiveness that may overwhelm newcomers; recovery language may skew toward AA's Big Book tradition over NA's Basic Text equally.

Important Disclaimers

This document is a companion reference — not a replacement for fellowship, sponsorship, or professional support. The 12 steps are best worked with an experienced sponsor in the context of a real recovery community.

If you are in crisis, please call 988 (USA) or visit a local emergency room. Long-term sobriety is built in community — please find your people.

On Limitations

This document cannot replace: lived community experience; personalized sponsorship; the nuance of in-person meetings; professional clinical or psychological support; the full text of official AA and NA literature.
