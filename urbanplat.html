Autonomy as Counter-Platform

Urban Mechanics of Self-Organization in Spain's Autonomous Social Centers

Executive Summary

Across Spain's post-industrial cities, self-managed social centers (Centros Sociales Autogestionados - CSAs) have emerged as critical experiments in counter-platform urbanism—physical infrastructures that resist the algorithmic governance of platform capitalism by enacting alternative protocols for living, organizing, and producing culture.

This report analyzes three emblematic CSAs through the lens of platform studies: 🏛️ La Tabacalera (Madrid), 🌳 Can Masdeu (Barcelona), and 🔥 Can Vies (Barcelona). Each represents a distinct model of autonomy:

  • La Tabacalera: The Hybrid Cloud model—state-sanctioned but self-managed, now facing displacement through cultural gentrification.
  • Can Masdeu: The P2P/Fediverse model—ecological occupation achieving stability through distributed social/ecological value.
  • Can Vies: The Insurrectional Fork model—rebuilt after violent eviction, now threatened by bureaucratic absorption.

Core Argument: These spaces function as counter-platforms that "fork" abandoned state/capitalist infrastructure to create commons-based protocols. However, they face a critical threat not from police eviction but from enshittification—the gradual degradation of autonomy through institutionalization, urban planning, and bureaucratic co-optation.

Historical Lineage: CSAs inherit protocols from hacker culture (copyleft, forking), cypherpunk infrastructure (P2P networks, encryption), and Zapatista caracoles (consensus governance, "mandar obedeciendo"). They represent a convergence of digital and physical sovereignty experiments.

2025 Status: La Tabacalera closed for state-led renovation; Can Vies facing urban plan reclassification; Can Masdeu stable but in legal limbo. All three demonstrate that autonomy is protocol, not property—resilience depends on network effects, not legal recognition.

Context: From Homebrew to Caracoles

The urban autonomy experiments analyzed here emerge from a parallel evolution of digital and physical protocols for counter-sovereignty. Understanding this genealogy reveals CSAs as inheritors of hacker ethics, cypherpunk infrastructure, and Zapatista governance.

Phase 1: Hacker Ethos (1970s) — Sharing as Default

The Homebrew Computer Club (1975) established "fork everything" as foundational practice. Early hackers shared hardware schematics and code freely, creating the cultural DNA for both open-source software and autonomous spaces. Hackerspaces today directly echo CSA governance models.

Phase 2: Free Software Movement (1980s) — Copyleft Rebellion

Richard Stallman's GNU Project (1983) and the General Public License (GPL, 1989) formalized "viral freedom"—code that can be forked but must remain free. This protocol mirrors CSA principles: spaces can be used freely but cannot be enclosed. The Free Software Foundation created institutional scaffolding that autonomous spaces would later adapt.

Phase 3: Cypherpunk Infrastructure (1990s) — Privacy as Weapon

The Cypherpunk Manifesto (Timothy May, 1989) argued "crypto turns power to individuals." PGP encryption (1991) and Linux kernel (1991) provided full-stack autonomy: secure communication + forkable operating system. Zapatistas would later use this infrastructure for anonymous global organizing via Indymedia.

Phase 4: P2P Piracy (1999-2006) — Commons Explosion

Napster (1999), BitTorrent (2003), and The Pirate Bay (2006) demonstrated distributed resistance at scale. The "piracy ethic"—culture as commons, not commodity—directly informs CSA practices like La Tabacalera's no-money-filter policy and Can Masdeu's gift economy.

Phase 5: Zapatista Caracoles (1994-Present) — Physical DAOs

The EZLN uprising (Jan 1, 1994) created 38 autonomous municipalities governed through consensus assemblies. The five caracoles (2003) function as "offline DAOs"—decentralized autonomous organizations built on rotation, horizontal governance, and "mandar obedeciendo" (lead by obeying). As of October 2025, caracoles face military attacks but remain resilient, with communities expanding "resistances" despite siege conditions.

Bridge to CSAs: Spanish autonomous centers synthesize these protocols. They occupy buildings (physical forking), use consensus assemblies (Zapatista governance), distribute resources via gift economies (P2P networks), and resist through transparency (cypherpunk ethics).

Methodology: Platform Studies Lens

This analysis employs platform studies to read CSAs as socio-technical infrastructures rather than mere buildings. Drawing from Alexander Galloway's Protocol, Nick Srnicek's Platform Capitalism, and Tarleton Gillespie's work on platforms as mediators of power, we map CSAs onto five layers:

  1. Hardware: Physical building (abandoned asset as "bare metal")
  2. Operating System: Governance protocols (assemblies, consensus, rotation)
  3. Applications: Cultural/social programs (workshops, gardens, collectives)
  4. Users/Network: Neighborhoods, assemblies, translocal solidarities
  5. Threats: State/market interventions (eviction, renovation, urban planning)

This framework reveals CSAs as counter-platforms: infrastructures that resist platform capitalism's extraction logic by encoding commons-based protocols. Unlike Facebook or Uber (centralized, data-extractive), CSAs distribute governance and value horizontally.

Comparative Method

We analyze three CSAs selected for model diversity:

  • La Tabacalera: State-sanctioned cession (tests institutional hacking)
  • Can Masdeu: Tolerated occupation (tests ecological/social value generation)
  • Can Vies: Insurgent reconstruction (tests militant resilience)

Data sources include activist documentation, media coverage, urban planning documents, and theoretical synthesis. Analysis spans 1997-2025, with particular focus on 2014 (Can Vies riots) and 2023-2025 (La Tabacalera closure, Can Vies reclassification).

Findings: Three Models of Counter-Platform Autonomy

🏛️ Model 1: La Tabacalera — The Hybrid Cloud

Genesis: Opportunistic Symbiosis (2010)

The 18th-century Royal Tobacco Factory in Madrid's Lavapiés district fell into abandonment after privatization in the 2000s. When the Ministry of Culture's plan for a National Center for Visual Arts failed due to lack of funding, activists negotiated a cession agreement for 9,200m² of the complex. This created a unique "state-sanctioned squat"—legal access without ownership.

Platform Mechanics

  • Hardware: Historic state-owned building (infrastructure on loan)
  • OS: Horizontal assembly with consensus protocols; legal association as "wrapper" entity
  • Apps: 30+ collectives (theater, fablabs, bike repair, free psychology services)
  • Network: Lavapiés neighborhood + transient artist communities
  • Resilience Vector: Utility dependency—valuable to state as low-cost cultural animation

Philosophy: "Creative Chaos" as Protocol

La Tabacalera operated on strict anti-capitalist principles: no money filter (all activities free), radical openness (anyone can propose projects), horizontal governance. The physical space—graffitied walls, uneven floors—embodied "creative chaos" as deliberate aesthetic rejection of institutional polish.

Critical Vulnerability: Vendor Lock-In

In July 2023, the Ministry abruptly closed the building for €12.5M renovation to create a state-managed "Center for Artistic Production and Residencies"—professionalized artist housing selected via competition. The CSA accuses the Ministry of breaking prior agreements; neighborhood groups argue the plan will accelerate gentrification.

Threat Analysis: This represents cultural gentrification—clearing grassroots "chaos" for curated, depoliticized professionalism. The state's "owner refactor" exposes the fragility of contingent autonomy: when the landlord is the state, autonomy lasts only as long as it's useful.

🌳 Model 2: Can Masdeu — The P2P/Fediverse

Genesis: Non-Violent Standoff (2001-2002)

In December 2001, activists occupied a 17th-century farmhouse turned abandoned leper hospital on Barcelona's periphery. When 100 police arrived to evict 11 squatters in April 2002, a three-day standoff ensued. Neighbors blocked streets in solidarity, and a judge halted eviction, ruling: "The right to life is above the right to property."

Platform Mechanics

  • Hardware: 35-hectare valley between Collserola Natural Park and Nou Barris district
  • OS: Consensus + permaculture runtime—bi-weekly assemblies, low member turnover
  • Apps: 80+ community garden plots, weekly public workshops (PIC), solar/compost systems
  • Network: Nou Barris neighbors (gardeners) + Collserola park (ecological custodianship)
  • Resilience Vector: Proof-of-stake—social/ecological value makes eviction politically costly

Philosophy: Productive Occupation

Can Masdeu's legitimacy stems from tangible outputs: food grown, ecosystems restored, knowledge shared. By transforming derelict land into flourishing gardens and positioning themselves as de facto park custodians, the community makes an undeniable argument for their right to remain.

Symbiotic Resistance

The project's dual integration—social (neighborhood gardens) + ecological (park conservation)—creates multi-layered defense. To evict Can Masdeu would dismantle a beloved community resource and a valuable conservation project. This "rurban" strategy leverages peripheral location as strength, acting as porous membrane between city and nature.

Threat Analysis: Can Masdeu exists in legal limbo since 2005 court ruling granted owner's right to recover property while recognizing project's legitimacy. Stability depends on maintaining network effects—if gardens decline or community fractures, political protection weakens. Current focus: expanding model via "La Casa dels Futurs" campaign to occupy another abandoned hospital.

🔥 Model 3: Can Vies — The Insurrectional Fork

Genesis: Historical Reclamation + Hard Fork (1997 → 2014)

The building at Carrer dels Jocs Florals was CNT anarcho-syndicalist headquarters during the 1930s Spanish Revolution, seized by Franco, then abandoned. When occupied by neighborhood youth in 1997, it was deliberate historical reclamation. The defining moment came in May 2014:

  1. Eviction: City began demolition on May 26
  2. Riots: Five nights of street battles; protesters set excavator on fire
  3. Capitulation: City suspended demolition on May 30
  4. Rebuild: Community raised €90,000 via crowdfunding, collectively reconstructed

Platform Mechanics

  • Hardware: Historic CNT building (symbolic infrastructure)
  • OS: Anarcho-assembly + direct action—consensus as highest authority
  • Apps: Political collectives (anti-speculation, migrant rights, feminist groups), music spaces
  • Network: Sants neighborhood assembly + translocal solidarity networks
  • Resilience Vector: Viral backlash—state aggression catalyzed exponential support

The "Can Vies Effect"

This sequence—eviction → insurrection → capitulation → reconstruction—became a national symbol of successful defiance. The attempt to destroy the building backfired, transforming a local squat into a potent icon. The city won the battle but lost the war.

From Barricade to Bureaucracy (2025)

Having failed with force, authorities now employ bureaucratic absorption. In April 2025, Barcelona City Council proposed reclassifying Can Vies as "public facility" under General Urban Plan (PGM). This saves the building from demolition but opens door for city takeover of management—converting self-managed center into conventional community hall.

Threat Analysis: This is soft power co-optation. The building persists but autonomy faces neutralization. Final approval expected early 2026. Can Vies now fights "the war of the committee meeting" rather than the barricade. Meanwhile, noise complaints from immediate neighbors create internal tensions, though wider neighborhood support remains strong.

Protocol Wars: State as Distributed Sovereign

The state's interventions operate as protocolic control—not brute force but rule-enforcing infrastructure. Following Galloway's Protocol, we see three modes of state code:

La Tabacalera: Breaking API Changes

The 2023 closure is a breaking API change—the state unilaterally refactors the interface. What was "open assembly managing commons" becomes "professional artists selected by state." This mirrors how GitHub can enforce Terms of Service on forked repositories: the infrastructure owner retains ultimate control. Cultural gentrification operates by replacing grassroots protocols with institutional code.

Can Masdeu: Proof-of-Stake Consensus

Can Masdeu runs a proof-of-stake protocol where "stake" equals accumulated social/ecological value. The 2002 judicial ruling that "life > property" was achieved via public opinion consensus—the network validated the project's legitimacy. This creates an undeniable "blockchain" of value: eviction would destroy verifiable public goods (gardens, conservation work, community cohesion).

Can Vies: 51% Attack on State Authority

The 2014 riots functioned as a 51% attack on the state's eviction protocol. By physically halting demolition and mobilizing overwhelming popular support, the community demonstrated greater legitimacy than municipal authority. The subsequent crowdfunded rebuild was a hard fork—creating a stronger, more resilient version of the project. The state's chain was orphaned; the community's chain became canonical.

The Enshittification Trap

All three CSAs face what Cory Doctorow terms enshittification: the gradual degradation of platform quality as owners optimize for their own interests. The pattern:

  1. Phase 1: Platform attracts users with openness (state cedes building, tolerates squat)
  2. Phase 2: Users create value (cultural vitality, ecological restoration, political organizing)
  3. Phase 3: Owner extracts value by changing terms (renovation, reclassification, absorption)

The shift from eviction (hard kill) to institutionalization (soft kill) represents strategic evolution. States learn that violent repression creates martyrs (Can Vies Effect); bureaucratic absorption neutralizes resistance more effectively.

Network Effects & Interoperability

Strengths: CSAs demonstrate powerful cross-pollination. La Tabacalera's meta-collective model functions as an "app store" for autonomous initiatives. Can Masdeu bridges urban/ecological networks. Can Vies inspires translocal solidarity (the "Efecte" spread to other cities).

Weaknesses: Projects remain largely siloed (Madrid vs. Barcelona; no formal inter-CSA infrastructure). Potential exists for federated protocols: shared legal defense funds, knowledge repositories, emergency mutual aid networks. Future evolution might involve DAO-like structures for coordinated resistance while preserving local autonomy.

Systemic Structures: Code Dictates Control

CSAs expose the blackboxed power of urban planning as code. The state's "urban OS"—General Urban Plans (PGM), building classifications, heritage designations—embeds neoliberal assumptions (property rights > commons). Assemblies function as decompilers, making visible the otherwise-hidden protocols of urban governance.

The scalability paradox: viral success invites shutdown. La Tabacalera's cultural prominence made it valuable for state reappropriation. Can Vies' iconic status attracted bureaucratic attention. Solution may lie in modular replication—micro-squats, pop-up assemblies, distributed networks that are harder to target than monumental buildings.

Discussion: Counter-Platform Futures

Autonomy is Protocol, Not Property

The core insight: autonomy functions as executable code, not legal status. CSAs persist not through ownership but through enacted protocols—consensus assemblies, gift economies, horizontal coordination. This explains why Can Masdeu's legal limbo doesn't equal vulnerability; its protocol (gardens + ecology) generates sufficient network effects for stability.

Conversely, La Tabacalera's legal recognition provided no protection. When the state (the "platform owner") decided to refactor, the CSA's protocol was overwritten. Lesson: Formal agreements with states are sandboxed VMs—safe but revocable.

From Platform Capitalism to Platform Autonomy

Nick Srnicek's Platform Capitalism describes how digital platforms extract value via data monopolies and network effects. CSAs invert this logic:

  • Extraction → Commons: Value created (gardens, culture, knowledge) remains collectively owned
  • Algorithms → Assemblies: Governance via consensus, not code
  • Users → Commoners: Participants co-create infrastructure rather than consume services

However, CSAs face the same enshittification dynamics as digital platforms. States, like corporate owners, initially tolerate autonomy to extract legitimacy/value, then optimize for their own interests (gentrification, bureaucratic control).

The Zapatista Bridge

Zapatista caracoles provide the crucial offline DAO model. Their "mandar obedeciendo" (lead by obeying) protocol—rotating leadership, consensus decisions, community accountability—directly informs CSA governance. The October 2025 military siege demonstrates both resilience (caracoles endure despite attacks) and fragility (Oventik closed; expanding but under pressure).

Key difference: Zapatistas control territory (land-based sovereignty); CSAs occupy buildings (provisional autonomy). This explains divergent threat models: Zapatistas face military force; CSAs face bureaucratic absorption.

Scalar Challenges

CSAs excel at micro-scale (building-level autonomy) but struggle with meso-scale (citywide coordination) and macro-scale (national/transnational networks). Can Masdeu's "La Casa dels Futurs" campaign represents an attempt to scale via federated nodes—multiple autonomous sites with shared protocols.

Digital tools offer potential: blockchain for transparent mutual aid, mesh networks for communication resilience, Git-like systems for governance versioning. However, risks include techno-solutionism (assuming code can solve political problems) and surveillance vulnerabilities (digital traces enable state monitoring).

Recommendations: Hardening Counter-Platforms

For La Tabacalera (Closed/Displaced)

  • Mirror & Migrate: Archive all collective knowledge; establish pop-up nodes across Madrid
  • Legal Challenge: Sue for "open access" clause in renovation; demand transparency on Ministry's broken agreements
  • Narrative Offensive: Frame closure as cultural gentrification; document loss to neighborhood
  • Solidarity Network: Coordinate with other CSAs facing similar threats (Barcelona, Valencia)

For Can Masdeu (Stable Limbo)

  • Scale Nodes: Accelerate "La Casa dels Futurs" campaign; create federated eco-network
  • Document Value: Systematic measurement of ecological restoration + social impact (make proof-of-stake quantifiable)
  • Youth Integration: Expand children's programming to deepen intergenerational stakes
  • Legal Pressure: Demand formal recognition from park authorities as conservation partners

For Can Vies (Bureaucratic Threat)

  • Reject Merge: Public campaign against PGM reclassification; demand self-management clause
  • Neighborhood Mediation: Address noise complaints proactively; strengthen immediate neighbor relations
  • Preemptive Mobilization: Prepare 2014-style response if city attempts management takeover
  • International Solidarity: Leverage global icon status; coordinate with European squat networks

Cross-Platform Protocols

  • Inter-CSA Federation: Establish shared legal defense fund, knowledge repository, emergency mutual aid network
  • Platform Cooperativism: Develop digital tools for coordination (encrypted communication, transparent finances, assembly coordination)
  • Modular Replication: Create "CSA in a box" documentation for rapid deployment of new autonomous spaces
  • Transnational Learning: Exchange programs with Zapatista caracoles, Kurdish communes, other autonomy experiments

Strategic Principles

  1. Networks > Hardware: Invest in relationships (neighbors, movements, translocal solidarity) over building improvements
  2. Diversity of Tactics: No single model works; maintain spectrum from institutional negotiation to insurrectional resistance
  3. Transparency as Armor: Document everything; make governance, finances, decision-making radically visible
  4. Proactive Narrative: Control framing before crises; establish CSAs as essential urban infrastructure, not marginal squats

Reflexive: Bias & Limitations

Analytical Perspective

This analysis reflects a Western anarchist theoretical lens, privileging horizontal governance and anti-state autonomy. This may undervalue pragmatic engagement with institutions or overlook non-anarchist autonomy models (e.g., municipalism, participatory budgeting).

Digital Determinism Risk

The platform studies framework, while illuminating, risks imposing digital metaphors onto material struggles. Terms like "forking" and "proof-of-stake" may obscure the embodied, affective, and historically-specific dimensions of urban occupation. CSAs are not reducible to code.

Positionality

Analysis conducted from position of sympathetic observer rather than embedded participant. Limited access to internal assembly dynamics, interpersonal conflicts, or quotidian maintenance labor that sustains (or strains) these projects.

Temporal Limitations

Data current through October 2025. La Tabacalera renovation outcome, Can Vies PGM final approval, and Zapatista caracoles' response to military pressure remain unresolved. Analysis may be superseded by events.

Geographic Scope

Focus on Spanish CSAs limits generalizability. Different legal/cultural contexts (e.g., Germany's robust squat culture, UK's criminalization of squatting) produce distinct autonomy models. Comparative research across Europe/Global South needed.

Methodological Note

Analysis synthesizes secondary sources (media, activist documentation, theory) rather than primary ethnographic research. Depth achieved at expense of lived experience granularity.
